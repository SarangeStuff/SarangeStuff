Scalars and vectors

A scalar has only a size: time, mass, distance travelled, speed. A vector has a size and a direction: position, displacement, velocity, acceleration, force. In this course a vector is written with an arrow, \(\vec v\), and its size (the magnitude) is written without one, \(v\) or \(|\vec v|\).

You will meet two ways of writing the same vector:

\[ \vec v = \underbrace{(5.32\ \text{m/s},\ \measuredangle -117^\circ)}_{\text{magnitude, angle}} \qquad\text{or}\qquad \vec v = \underbrace{-2.44\,\hat i + 4.73\,\hat j\ \ \text{m/s}}_{\text{components}} \]

The angle form is the one your memos use for final answers. Angles are measured from the positive \(x\)-axis, anticlockwise positive. Converting between them is the bread and butter of the course:

\[ v_x = v\cos\theta,\quad v_y = v\sin\theta \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad v=\sqrt{v_x^2+v_y^2},\quad \theta=\tan^{-1}\!\frac{v_y}{v_x}\ \text{(check the quadrant!)} \]
Common slip

Your calculator’s \(\tan^{-1}\) only returns angles between −90° and +90°. A vector like \(-2.44\hat i + 4.73\hat j\) points up and to the left, so its angle is \(180^\circ - 62.7^\circ = 117.3^\circ\), not 62.7°. Sketch the vector before trusting the button.

The two triangle rules

Almost every vector diagram in this course is a triangle. You can measure it with a ruler, or solve it exactly with one of these two rules.

\[ \textbf{Sine rule:}\ \ \frac{a}{\sin A}=\frac{b}{\sin B}=\frac{c}{\sin C} \qquad\qquad \textbf{Cosine rule:}\ \ a^2=b^2+c^2-2bc\cos A \]
Which one, when?

Use the cosine rule when you know two sides and the angle between them. That is the typical relative velocity case: you know \(v_A\), \(v_B\) and the angle between their directions, and you want \(v_{B/A}\).

Use the sine rule when you know all three directions (so all three angles) and one side. That is the typical rigid-body case: one velocity is known completely and the other two are known only by direction. Most of Chapter 9 runs on the sine rule.

The one cross product you need

For flat (planar) motion, every angular velocity points along \(\hat k\), straight out of the page. Anticlockwise (CCW) is positive. The cross product with a position vector then has a very simple meaning:

\[ \omega\hat k \times (x\,\hat i + y\,\hat j) \;=\; \omega\,(-y\,\hat i + x\,\hat j) \]
Why this works

Look at the result: the vector \((x, y)\) has become \((-y, x)\). That is the same vector turned 90° anticlockwise, then stretched by \(\omega\). So “\(\vec\omega\times\vec r\)” just means turn \(\vec r\) a quarter-turn in the direction of spin, and scale it by \(\omega\). That is why the velocity of a point on a spinning body is always perpendicular to the line from the centre, and why its size is \(\omega r\). You will use this idea in Chapters 6, 9 and 10.

The handy unit-vector results are \(\hat k\times\hat i = \hat j\) and \(\hat k\times\hat j = -\hat i\).

Calculus as pictures

  • A derivative is a slope. \(v = ds/dt\) is the slope of the position–time graph.
  • An integral is an area. \(\Delta s = \int v\,dt\) is the area under the velocity–time graph.
  • The chain rule turns geometry into velocity. If \(x = 0.2\cos\theta\) and \(\theta\) changes with time, then \(\dot x = -0.2\sin\theta\,\dot\theta\). That one line is the whole idea of Chapter 7.

Dots mean time derivatives: \(\dot\theta = d\theta/dt = \omega\) and \(\ddot\theta = d^2\theta/dt^2 = \alpha\).

Units and significant figures

  • Angles in kinematics formulas are in radians. Convert revolutions per minute with \(\omega = N\cdot\frac{2\pi}{60}\). For example, 1500 rev/min = 157 rad/s.
  • Tutorials ask for 3 significant figures. Keep 4 or more in your working and round only at the end.
  • Check units at the end of every answer. \(\omega^2 r\) is (rad/s)²·m = m/s², and \(\omega r\) is m/s.
In one breath
  • Vectors carry a size and a direction. Sketch before using \(\tan^{-1}\).
  • Cosine rule: two sides and the angle between them. Sine rule: one side and all the angles.
  • \(\omega\hat k\times\vec r\) means turn \(\vec r\) 90° the way it spins and multiply by \(\omega\).
  • Derivative = slope, integral = area, chain rule = geometry into velocity.
