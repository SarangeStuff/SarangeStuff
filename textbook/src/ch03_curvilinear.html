Chapter 3 · Lectures L04–L08

Curved paths: three ways to look at the same motion

In this chapter
  • Rectangular (x–y) components and projectiles
  • Normal–tangential (n–t) components: the key to everything that turns
  • Polar (r–θ) components and the strange-looking Coriolis term
  • Cylindrical and spherical coordinates for 3D
The big idea

The motion is the same whichever coordinates you use. You only choose the coordinate system that makes your problem easy. Use x–y when the forces line up with fixed directions (gravity). Use n–t when you know the path and the speed along it. Use r–θ when something is measured from a fixed point, such as a rotating arm, a radar, or a slot.

One fact holds in every system: velocity is always tangent to the path. Acceleration usually is not, and the three systems are just three ways of splitting it into pieces.

Rectangular components (L04)

\[ \vec r = x\hat i + y\hat j \qquad \vec v = \dot x\,\hat i + \dot y\,\hat j \qquad \vec a = \ddot x\,\hat i + \ddot y\,\hat j \]

Because \(\hat i\) and \(\hat j\) never change direction, you differentiate each component on its own. A curved motion splits into two straight-line motions, each of which is a Chapter 2 problem.

Projectiles are the classic case. With no air resistance, \(a_x = 0\) and \(a_y = -g\).

\[ x = x_0 + v_{0x}t \qquad v_y = v_{0y} - gt \qquad y = y_0 + v_{0y}t - \tfrac12 g t^2 \qquad v_y^2 = v_{0y}^2 - 2g(y-y_0) \]
Why the horizontal speed never changes

Gravity pulls straight down. Nothing pushes sideways, so nothing changes the sideways velocity. The only thing the horizontal and vertical motions share is the clock. Time is the link you solve for, usually from the vertical equation, and then use in the horizontal one.

Worked example 3.1: a simple projectile

Tut02 Q1 uses the same method

A ball is kicked from level ground at 20 m/s, 35° above horizontal. Find its range and maximum height.

  1. Split the launch velocity: \(v_{0x} = 20\cos35^\circ = 16.38\) m/s and \(v_{0y} = 20\sin35^\circ = 11.47\) m/s.
  2. Time of flight: it lands when \(y = 0\) again, so \(0 = 11.47t - 4.905t^2 \Rightarrow t = 2.339\) s.The vertical motion decides when it lands. The horizontal motion only tells you where.
  3. Range: \(x = 16.38 \times 2.339 = 38.3\) m. Maximum height (where \(v_y = 0\)): \(H = 11.47^2/(2\times 9.81) = 6.71\) m.

Range 38.3 m, maximum height 6.71 m

Normal and tangential components (L05)

Ride along with the particle. One unit vector \(\hat e_t\) points forward along the path, and the other, \(\hat e_n\), points sideways towards the centre of curvature (the inside of the bend).

\[ \vec v = v\,\hat e_t \qquad\qquad \vec a = \underbrace{\dot v\,\hat e_t}_{a_t} + \underbrace{\frac{v^2}{\rho}\,\hat e_n}_{a_n} \]
Why two pieces, and why v²/ρ

Velocity can change in two ways: it can get longer or shorter (speed changes), or it can turn (direction changes). Each kind of change needs its own acceleration.

  • \(a_t = \dot v = dv/dt\) changes the speed. It is zero when you drive round a bend at a constant speed.
  • \(a_n = v^2/\rho\) changes the direction. It always points into the bend. Going twice as fast round the same bend turns the velocity twice as quickly and the velocity is twice as big, so \(a_n\) goes up four times. That is why \(v\) appears squared. A tighter bend (smaller \(\rho\)) turns you faster, so \(\rho\) is on the bottom.

Unless the speed is changing, \(a_n\) is the only acceleration, which is why you feel pushed outwards in a turning car.

For a path given as \(y(x)\), the radius of curvature is \(\rho = \frac{\left[1+(dy/dx)^2\right]^{3/2}}{|d^2y/dx^2|}\) (the hint in Tut02 Q2). On a circle, \(\rho\) is just the radius, and that special case drives all of rigid-body rotation (Chapter 6).

Worked example 3.2: a car on a bend

Like L05 Example 1

A car rounds a bend of radius 50 m at 20 m/s and is speeding up at 3 m/s². Find the size of its acceleration.

  1. \(a_t = \dot v = 3\) m/s² (given directly as the rate of change of speed).
  2. \(a_n = v^2/\rho = 20^2/50 = 8\) m/s², pointing to the centre of the bend.
  3. The two are perpendicular, so \(a = \sqrt{3^2 + 8^2}\).\(\hat e_t\) and \(\hat e_n\) are at right angles, so Pythagoras applies.

a = 8.54 m/s²

Polar coordinates (L06–L07)

Now describe the particle by its distance \(r\) from a fixed point O and the angle \(\theta\) of the line OP. The unit vectors are \(\hat e_r\) (straight out along OP) and \(\hat e_\theta\) (a quarter-turn anticlockwise from it).

\[ \vec v = \dot r\,\hat e_r + r\dot\theta\,\hat e_\theta \qquad\qquad \vec a = \big(\ddot r - r\dot\theta^2\big)\hat e_r + \big(r\ddot\theta + 2\dot r\dot\theta\big)\hat e_\theta \]
What each term means
  • \(\dot r\): sliding outwards along the arm. \(r\dot\theta\): being carried round by the arm, like a point at radius \(r\) on a wheel spinning at \(\dot\theta\).
  • \(\ddot r\): the outward slide speeding up.
  • \(-r\dot\theta^2\): the centripetal term (the \(v^2/\rho\) of a circle). It is negative because it points inwards, towards O.
  • \(r\ddot\theta\): the arm spinning faster.
  • \(2\dot r\dot\theta\): the Coriolis term. Two things happen when you slide outward on a spinning arm. You move to a bigger radius, where the same \(\dot\theta\) means a faster sideways speed. And your outward velocity is itself being swung round. Each effect contributes one \(\dot r\dot\theta\), so the term is \(2\dot r\dot\theta\).

Worked example 3.3: a collar on a rotating rod

Same set-up as Tut02 Q3–Q4

A rod spins at a constant \(\dot\theta = 2\) rad/s. A collar on it is at \(r = 0.5\) m and sliding outward at a steady \(\dot r = 0.3\) m/s. Find the collar’s velocity and acceleration.

  1. List the five numbers you need: \(r = 0.5\), \(\dot r = 0.3\), \(\ddot r = 0\), \(\dot\theta = 2\), \(\ddot\theta = 0\).Every polar question comes down to finding these five numbers. The formulas do the rest.
  2. \(v_r = 0.3\) m/s and \(v_\theta = 0.5\times2 = 1.0\) m/s, so \(v = \sqrt{0.3^2+1.0^2} = 1.04\) m/s.
  3. \(a_r = 0 - 0.5(2)^2 = -2.0\) m/s² (inwards) and \(a_\theta = 0 + 2(0.3)(2) = 1.2\) m/s², so \(a = 2.33\) m/s².Even with nothing speeding up, there are two accelerations: the inward centripetal one, and the Coriolis one from sliding outward on a spinning rod.

v = 1.04 m/s, a = 2.33 m/s²

3D: cylindrical and spherical (L08)

Cylindrical coordinates are polar coordinates with a height \(z\) added. Use them for helices (the roller coaster in L04) and anything turning around a vertical axis while rising.

\[ \vec v = \dot r\,\hat e_r + r\dot\theta\,\hat e_\theta + \dot z\,\hat k \qquad \vec a = (\ddot r - r\dot\theta^2)\hat e_r + (r\ddot\theta + 2\dot r\dot\theta)\hat e_\theta + \ddot z\,\hat k \]

Spherical coordinates \((R, \theta, \phi)\) suit radar antennas and anything pointing into the sky, like latitude (\(\phi\), measured up from the horizontal plane) and longitude (\(\theta\)). The velocity, as in L08, is

\[ \vec v = \dot R\,\hat e_R + R\dot\theta\cos\phi\,\hat e_\theta + R\dot\phi\,\hat e_\phi \]

and the acceleration components are

\[ a_R = \ddot R - R\dot\phi^2 - R\dot\theta^2\cos^2\phi \qquad a_\theta = \frac{\cos\phi}{R}\frac{d}{dt}\!\left(R^2\dot\theta\right) - 2R\dot\theta\dot\phi\sin\phi \qquad a_\phi = \frac1R\frac{d}{dt}\!\left(R^2\dot\phi\right) + R\dot\theta^2\sin\phi\cos\phi \]

You are not expected to derive these. Learn to list \(R, \dot R, \ddot R, \theta, \dot\theta, \ddot\theta, \phi, \dot\phi, \ddot\phi\) carefully, just as for polar coordinates, and then substitute.

In one breath
  • Velocity is always tangent to the path. Choose coordinates to suit the problem.
  • x–y: components are independent, and time links them.
  • n–t: \(a_t = \dot v\) changes the speed and \(a_n = v^2/\rho\) turns the velocity into the bend.
  • r–θ: list the five numbers, then use \((\ddot r - r\dot\theta^2)\) and \((r\ddot\theta + 2\dot r\dot\theta)\).
