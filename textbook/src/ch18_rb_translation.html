Chapter 18 · Lectures L33–L34 (rectilinear translation)

Rigid bodies that slide without turning

In this chapter
  • The three equations of planar rigid-body motion
  • Why a translating body still needs a moment equation
  • Taking moments about a smart point, \(\sum M_P = m a_G d\)
  • The boom on the bakkie (review lecture)

A rigid body can rotate as well as move, so it needs one more equation than a particle. For planar motion there are three:

\[ \sum F_x = m(a_G)_x \qquad \sum F_y = m(a_G)_y \qquad \sum M_G = I_G\,\alpha \]

The force equations use the acceleration of the mass centre G. The moment equation is taken about G, with \(I\) meaning \(I_G\) (L33 marks this “NB!”).

There is also a very useful alternative. Take moments about any point P, and include the moment of the kinetic diagram on the right-hand side:

\[ \sum M_P = I_G\,\alpha + m a_G\,d \]

Here \(d\) is the perpendicular distance from P to the line of \(m\vec a_G\).

Why the extra term

The forces on the FBD and the \(m\vec a_G\) (plus \(I_G\alpha\)) on the kinetic diagram are equivalent, so they must have the same moment about every point, not just about G. About G, \(m\vec a_G\) has no moment, which leaves \(I_G\alpha\). About any other point it does, \(m a_G d\). Pick P where two unknown forces meet, and both drop out of the equation at once.

Translation: no rotation, but moments still matter

In translation, \(\omega = \alpha = 0\), so \(\sum M_G = 0\). That does not make the moment equation useless. It is what decides how the load is shared between wheels, pins and cables. About any other point P, \(\sum M_P = m a_G d\).

Worked example 18.1: a wheelie

L33 Think-Pair-Share 1

A motorbike and rider (295 kg) hold a wheelie while accelerating at 5 m/s². Find the friction force on the back wheel.

  1. The front wheel is off the ground, so the back wheel’s friction is the only horizontal force: \(F = ma = 295 \times 5\).The moment equation about G is what holds the wheelie at a steady angle. The horizontal force equation alone answers this question.

F = 1475 N

Worked example 18.2: the boom on the bakkie

Review lecture 1, Example 2

A uniform 4 m, 60 kg boom is pinned at A to the back of a bakkie, at 60° to the horizontal, and held by a cable from its top B to C, 2 m in front of A. The bakkie accelerates forwards at 5 m/s². Find the cable tension and the total pin force at A.

{{fig:k_boom_fbd}}={{fig:k_boom_kd}}
  1. Geometry: B is at \((-2, 3.464)\) m from A. The cable angle at C is \(\theta = \tan^{-1}\dfrac{4\sin60^\circ}{4\cos60^\circ + 2} = 40.9^\circ\). G is 1.0 m behind and 1.732 m above A.
  2. Moments about A, clockwise positive. This removes \(A_x\) and \(A_y\) in one go:
    \[ T\,(2\sin40.9^\circ) - 589(1.0) = \underbrace{300 \times 1.732}_{m a_G d} \]
    so \(T = 846\) N.The cable’s moment arm about A is the perpendicular distance from A to the line BC, which is \(AC\sin\theta\). The kinetic diagram’s moment is \(ma\) times the height of G above A.
  3. Horizontal: \(T\cos40.9^\circ - A_x = 300\), so \(A_x = 340\) N (acting backwards on the boom).
  4. Vertical (no vertical acceleration): \(A_y - 589 - T\sin40.9^\circ = 0\), so \(A_y = 1143\) N.

T = 846 N, A = √(340² + 1143²) = 1192 N (memo: 846 N and 1192 N)

In one breath
  • \(\sum F = m a_G\) and \(\sum M_G = I_G\alpha\), or \(\sum M_P = I_G\alpha + m a_G d\) about any P.
  • Translation: \(\alpha = 0\), but moments still decide the reactions.
  • Take moments where the unknowns meet.
  • Practice: M&K 6/10 in L33 (T = 24.5 N, FA = 32.9 N).
