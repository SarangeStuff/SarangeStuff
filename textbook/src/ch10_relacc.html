Relative acceleration: drawing the acceleration diagram

In this chapter
  • Why the relative acceleration has two parts, and which part is always known
  • The accelerations of pins, sliders and rolling wheels
  • The “two chains” recipe for an acceleration diagram that always closes
  • Worked examples: ladder, engine, and the 2016, 2021 and 2022 exam questions
  • Rolling without slipping, and a slider-crank you can turn by hand

The equation, and why it has two relative parts

Differentiate \(\vec v_B = \vec v_A + \vec v_{B/A}\) and you get \(\vec a_B = \vec a_A + \vec a_{B/A}\). Chapter 9 showed that, seen from A, point B moves on a circle of radius \(r_{AB}\). Chapter 3 showed that motion on a circle has two acceleration components. Put those together:

\[ \vec a_B = \vec a_A + (\vec a_{B/A})_n + (\vec a_{B/A})_t \qquad \begin{cases} (a_{B/A})_n = \omega^2 r_{AB} & \text{from B towards A} \\[2pt] (a_{B/A})_t = \alpha\, r_{AB} & \perp AB \end{cases} \]
Why the normal part is always known (and why velocities come first)

The normal part \(\omega^2 r\) depends only on \(\omega\) (from the velocity diagram) and the bar length, and it always points along the bar towards the reference point. So once the velocity diagram is solved, every normal component in the problem is fully known: size and direction. That is why the exam always asks for the velocity diagram first, and why the amber arrows in this book are always drawn before the green ones.

The tangential part \(\alpha r\) is perpendicular to the bar (direction known) but its size contains the unknown \(\alpha\). It plays exactly the role that \(\vec v_{B/A}\) played in Chapter 9.

In vector form (L16): \(\vec a_B = \vec a_A - \omega^2\vec r_{B/A} + \vec\alpha\times\vec r_{B/A}\). The \(\omega^2\) term is not a cross product. It is \(\omega^2\) times \(\vec r_{B/A}\), with a minus sign, which is just the arrow from B back towards A.

Accelerations of the usual suspects

PointNormal partTangential part
Pin on a crank about a fixed pivot\(\omega^2 r\), towards the pivot (known once \(\omega\) is known)\(\alpha r\), ⟂ crank. Zero if \(\omega\) is constant. Unknown if \(\alpha\) is what you want.
Slider in a straight guideOnly one part, along the guide. Size usually unknown. (A straight path has no normal acceleration.)

The recipe: two chains that meet

An acceleration diagram has more arrows than a velocity diagram, but there are still only two unknowns. The trick is to organise the equation so that each unknown comes last in a chain of arrows that starts at o.

\[ \underbrace{(\vec a_C)_n + (\vec a_C)_t}_{\text{chain 1: what C does on its own path}} \;=\; \underbrace{\vec a_B + (\vec a_{C/B})_n + (\vec a_{C/B})_t}_{\text{chain 2: reach C via B}} \]
  1. Solve the velocity diagram first. Write down every \(\omega\) with its sense.Every \(\omega^2 r\) term needs these.
  2. Write the equation with the unknown point on the left, splitting every acceleration on a curved path into \(n\) and \(t\). This is the form the memos tick:
    \[ (\vec a_C)_n + (\vec a_C)_t = (\vec a_B)_n + (\vec a_B)_t + (\vec a_{C/B})_n + (\vec a_{C/B})_t \]
  3. Audit table: size and direction of every term. Calculate all the normal parts now. Expect exactly two question marks, both of them sizes of perpendicular terms (or of a slider’s acceleration).
  4. Choose and state a scale, for example 1 mm = 2 m/s².
  5. Chain 2 first: from o, draw the known parts of \(\vec a_B\) head to tail, then \((\vec a_{C/B})_n\) from its tip, pointing from C towards B.You can only draw a vector with an arrowhead when you know both its size and direction, so all the fully known vectors go down first.
  6. Through the end of chain 2, draw a long line perpendicular to BC, the direction of \((\vec a_{C/B})_t\).
  7. Chain 1: from o, draw the known part of C’s own acceleration. For a pin on a crank that is \((\vec a_C)_n\), towards that crank’s pivot. For a slider there is no known part, so start at o itself.
  8. Through the end of chain 1, draw a long line in the unknown direction: ⟂ the crank for \((\vec a_C)_t\), or along the guide for a slider.
  9. The two lines cross at the tip of \(\vec a_C\). Put arrowheads on both unknown vectors, finishing at the crossing.Both chains describe the same \(\vec a_C\), so they must end at the same point. The crossing is the only point that lies on both lines.
  10. Measure the two unknowns, convert with the scale, and get \(\alpha = (a_{\text{rel}})_t / r\). Find the sense with pin-and-push on the green arrow. Measure \(\vec a_C\) itself (o to the crossing) if it is asked for.
Normal points to the pin you measure from

\((\vec a_{C/B})_n\) points from C towards B, the letter after the slash. For a crank about a fixed pivot P, \((\vec a_C)_n\) points from C towards P. Every normal acceleration points inwards, towards the centre the point is circling (relative to that reference point).

Worked example 10.1: the ladder, first acceleration diagram

Continues worked example 9.1

The same ladder, with A moving right at a constant 3 m/s (so \(\vec a_A = 0\)). Find \(\vec a_B\) and \(\alpha_{AB}\) at \(\theta = 30^\circ\).

  1. From Chapter 9: \(\omega_{AB} = 4.00\) rad/s CCW.
  2. \((a_{B/A})_n = \omega^2 r = 4^2(1.5) = 24.0\) m/s², pointing from B towards A, so down and to the right along the bar.
  3. Equation: \(\vec a_B = \underbrace{\vec a_A}_{0} + (\vec a_{B/A})_n + (\vec a_{B/A})_t\). The unknowns are the size of \(\vec a_B\) (direction vertical) and the size of \((\vec a_{B/A})_t\) (direction ⟂ AB).
  1. Measured, or from components: \(a_B = 48.0\) m/s² and \((a_{B/A})_t = 41.6\) m/s².
  2. \(\alpha = 41.6/1.5 = 27.7\) rad/s². The green arrow points down-left at B. Pin A and push B that way: anticlockwise.

aB = 48.0 m/s² ↓, αAB = 27.7 rad/s² CCW

α has the same sense as ω, so the ladder’s rotation is speeding up as it falls flatter, which matches what you see when a ladder slips.

Worked example 10.2: the engine’s piston acceleration

L16 Example 2 (continues worked example 9.2)

Same slider-crank: crank 125 mm at a constant 1500 rev/min CW, rod 350 mm, \(\theta = 60^\circ\). Find \(\vec a_A\) and \(\alpha_{AB}\).

  1. From the velocity diagram: \(\omega_{OB} = 157.1\) rad/s CW and \(\omega_{AB} = 29.5\) rad/s CCW.
  2. B is on a crank with constant speed, so \((a_B)_t = 0\) and \(a_B = (a_B)_n = 157.1^2(0.125) = 3084\) m/s², from B towards O.
  3. \((a_{A/B})_n = \omega_{AB}^2\, r_{AB} = 29.5^2(0.350) = 305\) m/s², from A towards B (up the rod at 18°).The L16 slide prints 0.250 m here, but 305 only comes out with the real rod length, 0.350 m. That is a typo on the slide; the answer 305 is right.
  4. Unknowns: the size of \(\vec a_A\) (horizontal) and the size of \((\vec a_{A/B})_t\) (⟂ AB).
  1. Component check. The \(y\)-equation contains only one unknown, because \(\vec a_A\) is horizontal:
    \[ y:\ 0 = -3084\sin60^\circ + 305\sin18^\circ + 0.951\,(a_{A/B})_t \;\Rightarrow\; (a_{A/B})_t = 2710\ \text{m/s}^2 \]\[ x:\ a_A = 1542 + 290 - 0.309(2710) = 994\ \text{m/s}^2 \]
    Picking the equation in which one unknown has no component is the fastest way to solve these by hand.
  2. \(\alpha_{AB} = 2710/0.350 = 7740\) rad/s². The green arrow points up-left at A. Pin B and push A (the left end) upwards: clockwise.

aA = 994 m/s² →, αAB = 7740 rad/s² CW

Worked example 10.3: the 2016 exam, parts (c) and (d)

Draw the relative acceleration diagram of bar BD and use it to find \(\alpha_{BD}\). (AB turns at a constant 10 rad/s CW.)

From the velocity diagram: \(\omega_{BD} = 8.00\) rad/s CCW and \(\omega_{DE} = 24.0\) rad/s CCW. D is the point whose acceleration is not fully known, so put it on the left:

TermSizeDirection
\((\vec a_D)_n = \omega_{DE}^2 r_{DE}\)✓ \(24^2(0.2) = 115.2\)✓ D → E (→)
\((\vec a_D)_t = \alpha_{DE} r_{DE}\)?✓ ⟂ DE (vertical)

Worked example 10.4: the 2022 exam, parts (b) and (c)

Continues worked example 9.4 · exam scale 1 m/s² : 20 cm

With \(\omega_{AB} = 3\) rad/s and \(\alpha_{AB} = 2\) rad/s², both CCW, draw the acceleration diagram for bar BC and find the acceleration of block C.

From the velocity diagram: \(\omega_{BC} = 1.125\) rad/s CCW. The new feature is that AB is speeding up, so B has a tangential part as well.

\[ \vec a_C \;=\; (\vec a_B)_n + (\vec a_B)_t + (\vec a_{C/B})_n + (\vec a_{C/B})_t \]
TermSize (m/s²)Direction
\((\vec a_B)_n = \omega_{AB}^2 r_{AB}\)✓ \(9(0.07) = 0.63\)✓ B → A (↓)
\((\vec a_B)_t = \alpha_{AB} r_{AB}\)✓ \(2(0.07) = 0.14\)✓ ⟂ AB, ← (pin A, spin CCW)
\((\vec a_{C/B})_n = \omega_{BC}^2 r_{BC}\)✓ \(1.125^2(0.13) = 0.1645\)✓ C → B (22.6° from vertical)

\(\vec a_C\)?✓ along the slope (36.9°)
  1. Measure \(\vec a_C\) from o to the crossing: 0.547 m/s² (10.9 cm at 20 cm per m/s²). The memo accepts anything between 0.492 and 0.602 m/s², so neat drawing within 5% gets full marks.
  2. The green arrow is 0.39 m/s², so \(\alpha_{BC} = 0.39/0.13 = 3.0\) rad/s². Pin B and push C up-left: clockwise. (This part was not asked, but it is a useful check.)

aC = 0.547 m/s², directed down the slope

The mark people lose here

Because AB has an angular acceleration, \((\vec a_B)_t = \alpha_{AB} r_{AB}\) is not zero. The memo gives it its own tick “(mag. & dir.)”. Always ask whether the driving crank’s \(\omega\) is constant. If it is not, B has a tangential part.

Worked example 10.5: the 2021 exam, a bar with ω = 0

Final exam 2021 Q5(b)–(c) · continues worked example 9.5

AB turns at a constant 6 rad/s CW. Draw the relative acceleration diagram of BDE, find \(\alpha_{BDE}\), then find the acceleration of E.

  1. From the velocity diagram: \(\omega_{BDE} = 0\) and \(\omega_{DC} = 3.00\) rad/s CW.
  2. Equation for D:
    \[ (\vec a_D)_n + (\vec a_D)_t = (\vec a_B)_n + (\vec a_B)_t + (\vec a_{D/B})_n + (\vec a_{D/B})_t \]
  3. Terms: \((a_D)_n = 3^2(0.18) = 1.62\) ↓ (towards C). \((a_D)_t\): horizontal, size ?. \((a_B)_n = 6^2(0.09) = 3.24\) ↓ (towards A). \((a_B)_t = 0\) (constant ω). \((a_{D/B})_n = 0^2(0.242) = 0\). \((a_{D/B})_t\): ⟂ BD, size ?.With \(\omega_{BDE} = 0\), the normal relative term vanishes. This is the one situation where it does.
  1. The vertical components give \(-1.62 = -3.24 + (a_{D/B})_t\cos21.8^\circ\), so \((a_{D/B})_t = 1.745\) m/s².
  2. \(\alpha_{BDE} = 1.745/0.242 = 7.21\) rad/s². The green arrow points up-left at D. Pin B and push D (up-right of B) that way: anticlockwise.
  3. Part (c): E is on the same bar, so \(\vec a_E = \vec a_B + (\vec a_{E/B})_n + (\vec a_{E/B})_t\), with \((a_{E/B})_n = 0\) and \((a_{E/B})_t = 7.21(0.485) = 3.49\) m/s² ⟂ BE. In components:
    \[ \vec a_E = (0, -3.24) + 3.49(-0.371,\ 0.928) = (-1.30,\ 0)\ \text{m/s}^2 \]
    Once you know \(\alpha\) for a bar, any other point on it follows from one more relative acceleration equation.

αBDE = 7.2 rad/s² CCW, aE = 1.30 m/s² ← (memo: 7.2 rad/s² and 1.3 m/s² ←)

Rolling without slipping, and gears

For a wheel of radius \(r\) rolling on a fixed surface (L17):

\[ v_O = \omega r \qquad a_O = \alpha r \ (\text{parallel to the surface}) \qquad a_{\text{contact}} = \omega^2 r \ (\text{towards }O) \]
Why the contact point accelerates

The contact point is at rest for an instant, but a moment earlier it was coming down and a moment later it is lifting off. Its path is a cusp of a cycloid, and that sharp change of direction needs an acceleration of \(\omega^2 r\) straight up into the wheel. This is why you can never use the IC for accelerations.

Worked example 10.6: a gear rolling on a rack

L17 Example 1

A gear of radius 0.3 m rolls to the right on a fixed rack with \(\omega = 12\) rad/s and \(\alpha = 6\) rad/s², both clockwise. Find the acceleration of point A at the top.

  1. Use the centre O as the reference point, because its acceleration is known: \(a_O = \alpha r = 6(0.3) = 1.8\) m/s² →.
  2. \((a_{A/O})_n = \omega^2 r = 144(0.3) = 43.2\) m/s², from A towards O (↓). \((a_{A/O})_t = \alpha r = 1.8\) m/s², ⟂ OA. For CW α at the top of the wheel that is →.
  3. Add head to tail: \(\vec a_A = (1.8 + 1.8)\hat i - 43.2\hat j\).

aA = 43.3 m/s² at ∡ −85.2°

Two gears in mesh share the same \(a_t\) at the contact point (\(\alpha_1 r_1 = \alpha_2 r_2\)), but each has its own \(a_n = \omega^2 r\), so their total accelerations at the contact differ (L17, “bodies in contact”).

Common slips with acceleration diagrams
  • Starting on accelerations before the velocities are solved. Every normal term needs an \(\omega\).
  • Pointing a normal arrow the wrong way. It always points towards the reference point or the pivot.
  • Forgetting \((a_B)_t\) when the driving crank has an \(\alpha\), or putting one in when \(\omega\) is constant.
  • Drawing the tangential term along the bar. It is always ⟂ to the bar, like the relative velocity.
  • Squaring the wrong thing: it is \(\omega^2 r\), not \((\omega r)^2\).
  • Using the IC to find accelerations.
  • Not stating the sense of \(\alpha\) (CW/CCW). It is a separate mark.
In one breath
  • \(\vec a_C = \vec a_B + (\vec a_{C/B})_n + (\vec a_{C/B})_t\). The normal part is \(\omega^2 r\) towards B (known after the velocities). The tangential part is \(\alpha r\), ⟂ BC (direction only).
  • Points on cranks have \(n\) and \(t\) parts. Sliders have one part, along the guide.
  • Two chains from o, unknowns last. Their direction lines cross at the answer.
  • \(\alpha = (a_{\text{rel}})_t/r\). Sense by pin-and-push on the green arrow.
