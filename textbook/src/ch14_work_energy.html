Work, energy and power

In this chapter
  • What work is, and which forces do none
  • The work of weight and of a spring
  • \(T_1 + \sum U = T_2\), and the potential-energy form \(T_1 + V_1 + U' = T_2 + V_2\)
  • Power and efficiency
The big idea

Energy is bookkeeping. Kinetic energy is the account balance. Work is a deposit (positive) or a withdrawal (negative). Gravity and springs can be treated as savings accounts (potential energy) that you can draw back from. Because it is all scalars, you never need to resolve directions, and forces that do no work simply disappear from the problem.

Work

\[ U_{1-2} = \int \vec F\cdot d\vec r = \int F\cos\alpha\,ds \qquad\text{(constant force: } U = F\cos\alpha\;\Delta s\text{)} \]

Only the part of the force along the motion does work. That gives three quick rules:

  • Force along the motion: positive work. Force against it (friction, braking): negative work.
  • Zero work: a force perpendicular to the motion (the normal force on a fixed surface), or a force at a point that does not move (a fixed pin, or the contact point of a wheel rolling without slip).
  • Weight: \(U_g = -mg\,\Delta y\). It is negative going up and positive going down, and only the height change matters, not the path.
  • Linear spring: \(U_s = \tfrac12 k(x_1^2 - x_2^2)\), where \(x\) is measured from the unstretched length.
Common slip

Using a change in length instead of the stretch. The spring formula needs the stretch at each end of the motion, measured from the spring’s natural length. A spring that is already compressed at the start (Supp 2025, Chapter 16) starts with \(x_1 \neq 0\).

The work–energy principle

\[ T_1 + \sum U_{1-2} = T_2 \qquad\text{where}\qquad T = \tfrac12 m v^2 \]
Where it comes from

Take the tangential part of Newton’s law, \(\sum F_t = m\,dv/dt\), and use the Chapter 2 trick \(a\,ds = v\,dv\): \(\sum F_t\,ds = m v\,dv\). Integrate along the path and the left side is the work, while the right side is \(\tfrac12mv_2^2 - \tfrac12mv_1^2\). So it is Newton’s law, integrated along the path, with time removed.

Move gravity and springs across to the energy side and you get the form L26 uses most:

\[ T_1 + V_1 + U'_{1-2} = T_2 + V_2 \qquad V_g = mgy\ (\text{from a datum you choose}) \qquad V_e = \tfrac12 kx^2 \]

\(U'\) is the work of everything except gravity and springs (friction, applied forces, motors). If \(U' = 0\), energy is conserved: \(T_1 + V_1 = T_2 + V_2\).

Common slip

Counting gravity twice, once as \(U_g\) and again as \(V_g\). Choose one bookkeeping style per problem.

Worked example 14.1: a collar dropping onto a spring

L25 Example 1

A 6 kg collar is released from rest and falls onto a spring (\(k = 12\) kN/m). Find its speed when the spring is compressed 50 mm, given that the collar has then fallen 0.55 m in total.

  1. States: 1 is the release (\(T_1 = 0\)), and 2 is at 50 mm compression.Energy compares two positions, so name them first.
  2. Weight: \(U_g = -mg\Delta y = -6(9.81)(-0.55) = +32.37\) J (it fell, so gravity did positive work).
  3. Spring: \(U_s = \tfrac12(12\,000)(0^2 - 0.05^2) = -15.0\) J (the spring resists).
  4. \(0 + 32.37 - 15.0 = \tfrac12(6)v^2\).

v = 2.41 m/s downwards

Power and efficiency (L27)

\[ P = \frac{dU}{dt} = \vec F\cdot\vec v = Fv\cos\theta \qquad\qquad \varepsilon = \frac{P_{\text{out}}}{P_{\text{in}}} < 1 \]

Power is how fast work is done, in watts (1 W = 1 J/s). Efficiency compares what you get out with what you put in. Friction inside the machine always takes a share, so \(\varepsilon\) is less than 1.

Worked example 14.2: winching a log

L27 Example 1

A winch pulls a 360 kg log up a 30° slope at a constant 1.2 m/s. \(\mu_k = 0.5\) and the winch efficiency is 0.80. Find the motor’s power input.

  1. Constant speed, so \(a = 0\), and the forces balance (Chapter 12 with \(ma = 0\)).
  2. \(N = mg\cos30^\circ = 3058\) N, so friction is \(0.5N = 1529\) N.
  3. Along the slope: \(T = 1529 + mg\sin30^\circ = 3295\) N.
  4. \(P_{\text{out}} = Tv = 3295 \times 1.2 = 3.95\) kW, and \(P_{\text{in}} = P_{\text{out}}/0.8\).

Pin = 4.94 kW

A quick one in the same style (L27 Example 2): an escalator lifts 30 people per minute (65 kg each) up 7 m. Useful power is \(\tfrac{30}{60}(65)(9.81)(7) = 2.23\) kW. With a 3 kW motor, \(\varepsilon = 0.744\).

In one breath
  • Work = force × distance along it. Normal forces, fixed pins and rolling contacts do no work.
  • \(T_1 + V_1 + U' = T_2 + V_2\), with \(V_g = mgy\) and \(V_e = \tfrac12kx^2\) (\(x\) from the unstretched length).
  • Use it for speed versus position. It cannot find forces perpendicular to the path.
  • \(P = Fv\), \(\varepsilon = P_{\text{out}}/P_{\text{in}}\).
