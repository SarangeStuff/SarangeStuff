Chapter 19 · Lecture L32 (rigid-body rotation I–II)

Rigid bodies spinning about a fixed pivot

In this chapter
  • The equations in n–t form, using Chapter 6’s kinematics
  • Why \(\sum M_O = I_O\alpha\) works about the pivot
  • “An instant after release”: what that phrase gives you
  • Two worked examples, including the I-beam from L32

Every point of the body moves on a circle around the fixed axis O, including the mass centre G at radius \(\bar r\). So G’s acceleration has the two parts from Chapter 6, and the force equations are best written in n–t components:

\[ \sum F_n = m\bar r\omega^2 \ (\text{towards }O) \qquad \sum F_t = m\bar r\alpha \qquad \sum M_O = I_O\,\alpha \]
Why moments about the pivot use I_O

Take moments about O with the Chapter 18 rule: \(\sum M_O = I_G\alpha + m a_t \bar r\). The tangential part \(m\bar r\alpha\) has moment arm \(\bar r\), and the normal part points straight at O, so it has no moment. That gives \(\sum M_O = (I_G + m\bar r^2)\alpha = I_O\alpha\), by the parallel-axis theorem (Chapter 17). The moment equation about the pivot is the most useful one, because the unknown pin forces at O drop out.

Procedure (L32)

  1. Use n–t axes centred on O.
  2. Draw the FBD: weight, applied loads and the two pin components \(O_n\), \(O_t\).
  3. Draw the kinetic diagram: \(m\bar r\omega^2\) towards O, \(m\bar r\alpha\) ⟂ OG, and \(I_G\alpha\).
  4. Find \(\alpha\) from \(\sum M_O = I_O\alpha\) first, then the pin forces from the two force equations.
“An instant after release from rest”

This phrase means \(\omega = 0\), so every \(\omega^2\) term is zero. But \(\alpha\) is not zero: the body has just started to speed up. Every exam uses this phrase. It makes the normal equation trivial and leaves you with \(\alpha\) and the tangential force.

Worked example 19.1: a rod released from horizontal

The classic case

A uniform 4 kg rod, 1.2 m long, is pinned at one end and released from rest in the horizontal position. Find its initial angular acceleration and the pin force.

{{fig:k_rod_fbd}}={{fig:k_rod_kd}}
  1. \(I_O = \tfrac13mL^2 = 1.92\) kg·m².
  2. \(\sum M_O = I_O\alpha\): \(mg(L/2) = 1.92\,\alpha\), so \(\alpha = \dfrac{3g}{2L} = 12.3\) rad/s² clockwise.
  3. Normal: \(O_n = m\bar r\omega^2 = 0\).
  4. Tangential (down positive): \(mg - O_t = m\bar r\alpha = 4(0.6)(12.26)\), so \(O_t = 39.24 - 29.43\).G drops at \(\tfrac34 g\), so the pin only needs to hold a quarter of the weight. The pin force suddenly falls from \(mg\) to \(mg/4\) at the moment of release.

α = 12.3 rad/s² CW, pin force = 9.81 N (= mg/4) upwards

Worked example 19.2: the I-beam on a bracket

L32 Rotation II, Example 2

A 300 kg beam with \(k_O = 2.32\) m is pinned at O on a small bracket. G is 1.8 m along and 0.9 m across from O. The support at B is removed. Find the pin force at O at that instant.

  1. \(I_O = mk_O^2 = 300(2.32)^2 = 1615\) kg·m².
  2. \(\sum M_O = I_O\alpha\): the weight’s moment arm is the horizontal distance 1.8 m, so \(300(9.81)(1.8) = 1615\,\alpha\) and \(\alpha = 3.28\) rad/s² CW.
  3. Geometry of OG: \(\bar r = \sqrt{1.8^2 + 0.9^2} = 2.012\) m, at \(\beta = \tan^{-1}(0.9/1.8) = 26.6^\circ\).
  4. Tangential (⟂ OG): \(mg\cos\beta - F_{O,t} = m\bar r\alpha\), so \(F_{O,t} = 2632 - 1981 = 651\) N.
  5. Normal (along OG, \(\omega = 0\)): \(F_{O,n} - mg\sin\beta = 0\), so \(F_{O,n} = 1316\) N.Resolve the weight into the n and t directions, because those are the directions of G’s acceleration.

FO = √(651² + 1316²) = 1469 N (memo: 1469 N)

In one breath
  • G moves on a circle: \(\sum F_n = m\bar r\omega^2\), \(\sum F_t = m\bar r\alpha\).
  • \(\sum M_O = I_O\alpha\), with \(I_O = I_G + m\bar r^2\). Use it first, because the pin forces drop out.
  • “Instant after release”: \(\omega = 0\), \(\alpha \neq 0\).
