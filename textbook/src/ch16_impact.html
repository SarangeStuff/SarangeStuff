Chapter 16 · Lecture L30

Impact: collisions, bounces and the coefficient of restitution

In this chapter
  • Central impact: momentum plus \(e\)
  • Oblique impact: work along the line of impact and across it separately
  • Energy lost in an impact
  • A three-stage exam question: fall, impact, spring
The big idea

A collision has two unknowns (the two speeds afterwards) but momentum conservation gives only one equation. The second equation comes from how “bouncy” the collision is, measured by the coefficient of restitution \(e\).

Central impact

The line of impact runs through the two mass centres at the moment of contact. In central impact both bodies move along it.

\[ m_A v_{A1} + m_B v_{B1} = m_A v_{A2} + m_B v_{B2} \qquad\qquad e = \frac{v_{B2} - v_{A2}}{v_{A1} - v_{B1}} = \frac{\text{speed of separation}}{\text{speed of approach}} \]
  • \(e = 1\): perfectly elastic, no energy lost (an ideal that real collisions never quite reach).
  • \(e = 0\): perfectly plastic. The bodies stick together and the most energy possible is lost.
  • Typical values: steel 0.5–0.8, wood 0.4–0.6, glass about 0.94.
Why e uses relative speeds

How hard two bodies squash together depends only on how fast they close on each other, not on how fast either one moves over the ground. So the fair measure of bounciness compares the closing speed before with the opening speed after. Keep one direction positive for every velocity, and the signs take care of themselves.

Worked example 16.1: a quick central impact

L30 Think-Pair-Share 1

Block B (1 kg, 10 m/s →) squarely hits block A (3 kg, at rest). Afterwards A moves at 4 m/s →. Find \(v_{B2}\) and \(e\).

  1. Momentum (→ positive): \(1(10) + 0 = 1\,v_{B2} + 3(4)\), so \(v_{B2} = -2\) m/s.
  2. \(e = \dfrac{4 - (-2)}{10 - 0} = 0.6\).B was behind, so the separation speed is A’s velocity minus B’s.

vB2 = 2 m/s ←, e = 0.6

Worked example 16.2: a bouncing ball

L30 Example 1

A ball thrown horizontally at 2.4 m/s from 0.9 m high bounces off hard ground with \(e = 0.8\). Find the rebound speed and the height it reaches.

  1. Falling (Chapter 3 projectile): \(v_y^2 = 2(9.81)(0.9)\), so \(v_y = 4.20\) m/s down.
  2. Impact with the fixed ground: only the part along the line of impact (vertical) changes, \(v_{y}' = e\,v_y = 3.36\) m/s up. The horizontal 2.4 m/s is unchanged on a smooth surface.
  3. Rebound speed \(\sqrt{2.4^2 + 3.36^2}\), and height \(h = 3.36^2/(2\times9.81)\).

v = 4.13 m/s, h = 0.576 m

Oblique impact

Now the velocities are at an angle to the line of impact. Set up an \(n\) axis along the line of impact and a \(t\) axis across it (the contact plane). Then:

  • Along \(n\): the momentum of the system is conserved, and \(e\) applies, exactly as in central impact.
  • Along \(t\): with smooth surfaces there is no force across the contact, so each body keeps its own \(t\)-velocity.

That gives four equations for the four unknowns (two components for each body).

Worked example 16.3: two hockey pucks

L30 Example 2

Identical pucks: A moves at 6 m/s along the line of impact (\(x\)). B moves at 10 m/s, with \(-10\cos30^\circ\) along \(x\) and \(+5\) m/s across it. \(e = 0.75\).

  1. \(x\)-momentum (equal masses): \(6 - 8.66 = v_{Ax2} + v_{Bx2}\).
  2. Restitution: \(v_{Bx2} - v_{Ax2} = 0.75(6 + 8.66) = 11.0\).
  3. Solve: \(v_{Ax2} = -6.83\) m/s and \(v_{Bx2} = 4.17\) m/s.
  4. \(y\): A keeps 0 and B keeps 5 m/s, so \(v_B = \sqrt{4.17^2 + 5^2}\) at \(\tan^{-1}(5/4.17)\).

vA = 6.83 m/s ←, vB = 6.51 m/s at 50.2° to the x-axis

Worked example 16.4: two spheres, from a supp exam

Supp exam Q7 (Review lecture 1)

Sphere A (20 kg) moves → at 4 m/s. Sphere B (5 kg) moves ← at 15 m/s on a parallel path. \(e = 0.6\), and the line of impact is at 55.15° to the vertical. Find A’s velocity just after impact, relative to the line of impact.

Left: geometry. Right: A’s velocity split along and across the line of impact.

  1. Components along \(n\) (from A towards B): \(u_A = 4\sin55.15^\circ = 3.283\) and \(u_B = -15\sin55.15^\circ = -12.31\) m/s. Across \(n\): A has \(4\cos55.15^\circ = 2.286\) m/s, and keeps it.
  2. Momentum along \(n\): \(20(3.283) + 5(-12.31) = 20v_{A\parallel} + 5v_{B\parallel}\).
  3. Restitution: \(v_{B\parallel} - v_{A\parallel} = 0.6(3.283 + 12.31) = 9.355\).
  4. Solve: \(v_{A\parallel} = -1.707\) m/s (A bounces back along the line). Then \(v_A = \sqrt{1.707^2 + 2.286^2}\), at \(\tan^{-1}(2.286/1.707)\) to the line of impact.

vA = 2.85 m/s at 53.3° to the line of impact

Check this one with your lecturer

The review-slide memo uses exactly these equations but reports \((v_{A2})_\parallel = -1.855\) m/s, giving 2.94 m/s at 50.9°. Putting −1.855 back into its own restitution equation gives \(e = 0.65\), not 0.6. The value −1.707 satisfies both momentum and \(e = 0.6\). The method is what earns most of the marks, and it is identical either way.

Energy lost in an impact

Momentum survives a collision. Kinetic energy usually does not: \(\text{loss} = \sum T_1 - \sum T_2\), and it goes into heat, sound and permanent dents. So never use energy conservation across an impact unless you are told it is perfectly elastic.

Worked example 16.5: putty, block and springs

Supp exam 2025, Q5 (Review lecture 2)

2 kg of putty is dropped 2 m onto an 18 kg block resting on two springs (\(k = 1.2\) kN/m each). It sticks. Find the maximum additional deflection \(\delta\).

  1. Stage 1, the fall (energy): \(v_P = \sqrt{2(9.81)(2)} = 6.264\) m/s.
  2. Stage 2, the impact (momentum, plastic): \(2(6.264) = 20v_3\), so \(v_3 = 0.6264\) m/s.This is the lossy step: 39.2 J arrives, and only 3.9 J survives as kinetic energy.
  3. Before impact the springs are already compressed by the block: \(2kx_0 = 18g\), so \(x_0 = 0.0736\) m.The springs’ starting energy is not zero. Missing this is the most common way to lose marks on this question.
  4. Stage 3, the compression (energy, datum at the lowest point): \(T_3 + V_{g3} + V_{e3} = V_{e4}\), which is \(3.924 + 196.2\,\delta + 6.496 = 1200(\delta + 0.0736)^2\).
  5. Solve the quadratic and keep the positive root.

δ = 65.9 mm (memo: 65.9 mm)

In one breath
  • Along the line of impact: system momentum plus \(e = \) separation ÷ approach.
  • Across it (smooth): each body keeps its own velocity component.
  • Energy is lost in impacts (\(e < 1\)), so switch to momentum for the impact and back to energy afterwards.
