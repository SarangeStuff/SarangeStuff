The diagram question checklist

This list is built from the 2016, 2021 and 2022 memos. Each line matches something the markers tick.

  1. Geometry first. Bar lengths (Pythagoras) and angles (\(\tan^{-1}\)). Write them down: they are marks.
  2. Driving crank: \(v = \omega r\), with direction and a sketch arrow. Convert rev/min to rad/s.
  3. Velocity equation written out, e.g. \(\vec v_C = \vec v_B + \vec v_{C/B}\).
  4. Audit: size and direction of each term, known or not.
  5. Velocity diagram to the given scale, with arrows on every vector, labels, and angles marked.
  6. \(\omega\) of each bar = relative velocity ÷ length, with sense (CW or CCW).
  7. Acceleration equation with every \(n\) and \(t\) part written separately.
  8. Audit: every normal part calculated (\(\omega^2 r\)) with its direction (“towards ___”). Every tangential part as “\(\alpha r\), ⟂ ___, ?”.
  9. Acceleration diagram to scale: known arrows first, two direction lines, crossing, arrowheads on everything.
  10. Measure, convert, then \(\alpha\) = tangential ÷ length, with sense. Answers within about ±5% earn full marks.

Choosing a scale when none is given

Find the largest known vector and aim for 10–15 cm. For example, 0.63 m/s² at 1 m/s² : 20 cm is 12.6 cm. 3084 m/s² at 1 mm = 25 m/s² is 123 mm. Always write the scale beside the diagram. Draw thin, sharp lines; use set squares for perpendiculars and a protractor for angles. Practise with the course app (convect.uct.ac.za) and then on paper, because the exam is on paper.

If you get stuck

  • Count the unknowns. More than two means you have missed a known direction (a slider’s guide, a crank’s perpendicular) or need another equation for a different bar.
  • Check the IC for a fast sanity check of any \(\omega\) (Chapter 8).
  • Switch to components (vector algebra) if the diagram looks wrong. Pick the component direction in which one unknown disappears.
  • Parallel velocities on a bar mean \(\omega = 0\) for that bar, so its normal relative term is zero, but not its \(\alpha\).

The whole of Part I in four sentences

Velocity is the slope of position, and acceleration is the slope of velocity. On a curve, acceleration splits into a part that changes speed and a part, \(v^2/\rho\), that turns you into the bend. On a rigid body, any point’s motion is a reference point’s motion plus a spin about it, and that spin always moves the point perpendicular to the line joining them. Draw that as a triangle for velocities and a polygon for accelerations, and you can solve every mechanism in this course.
