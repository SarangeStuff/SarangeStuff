MEC2047S · Dynamics I · Part I: Kinematics (L01–L17)

Dynamics, gently.

A plain-language companion to your lecture notes. It explains what each formula means, why it has the shape it has, and how to draw velocity and acceleration diagrams that earn full marks.

Kinematics is the geometry of motion. It asks where things are, how fast they move and how that speed changes, and it never asks why. Forces come later, in Part II. Everything in this half of the course comes from one chain of ideas:

\[ \text{position} \;\xrightarrow{\;d/dt\;}\; \text{velocity} \;\xrightarrow{\;d/dt\;}\; \text{acceleration} \]

Integration walks the chain backwards. The rest of the course is about two choices: which coordinates to describe the motion in, and which reference point to measure it from. Once you see each topic as one of those two choices, the whole course hangs together.

The colour code in every diagram

  • Blue: an absolute vector you already know completely, such as the velocity of a pin on a driven crank.
  • Amber: a normal component, \(\omega^2 r\). Once the velocities are solved, this is always fully known.
  • Green: a vector perpendicular to a bar. You know its direction but not its size until the diagram closes.
  • Crimson: the absolute vector you are solving for, such as the speed of a slider.
  • Grey dashes: construction lines. They show a direction before you know where the vector ends.

How to use this book

  • Read with your lecture slides open. Each chapter names the lectures it covers, and most worked examples are the same ones from your slides, slowed right down.
  • Look for the green “Why” boxes. They explain the science behind a formula, so you can rebuild it in the exam if your memory fails.
  • Use the step-through diagrams. Click Next step (or tap a numbered step) to watch a velocity or acceleration diagram being built one line at a time, in the order you would draw it on paper.
  • Chapters 9 and 10 are the heart of the book. If you only have one evening, read those two and Chapter 11.

Why the diagrams matter so much

Look at the review lectures. The 2016, 2021 and 2022 exam questions all ask you to draw a relative velocity or acceleration vector diagram “to the scale given” and then measure an answer from it. In the 2022 memo, part (b), the acceleration diagram alone, is worth 9 marks. Ticks go to each vector’s magnitude and direction, to the angles, to “all arrows” and to “diagram correct”. This is a drawing skill as much as a maths skill, and it can be learned like a recipe. Chapters 9 and 10 give you that recipe.
