Pulleys: motion tied together by a rope

In this chapter
  • The rope-length method (position coordinates)
  • The quick “velocity line” method for pulleys
  • Why a pulley is secretly a rolling wheel
The big idea

A rope does not stretch. Its total length is a constant, so if you write that length in terms of the positions of the blocks and differentiate, the constant disappears. What remains links the velocities, and differentiating again links the accelerations.

Method 1: the rope-length equation

  1. Datums. Measure each moving thing’s position (\(s_A\), \(s_B\), …) from a fixed line, usually through the centre of a fixed pulley. Choose positive in the direction each thing moves (often “down”).
  2. Length. Add up the rope segments that change length. Leave out bits that never change, such as the arcs wrapped round pulleys. A pulley hanging in a loop of rope contributes its coordinate twice, once for each side of the loop.
  3. More than one rope? Write one length equation per rope, then eliminate the shared coordinate.
  4. Differentiate. Constants vanish. Watch the signs: a negative answer means opposite to your positive direction.
Why a pulley’s coordinate counts twice

If a moving pulley hangs in a loop of rope and drops by 1 cm, both rope segments beside it get 1 cm longer. That 2 cm has to come from somewhere else in the rope. This is where every factor of 2 in pulley problems comes from: the block on a moving pulley moves at half the speed of the free end.

Worked example 5.1: two ropes

L10 Example 2: keep the slide’s figure open

The rope at A is pulled down at 2 m/s. Find the speed of block B.

  1. Datum through the fixed top pulley, with all coordinates positive downwards. There are three moving things: the end A, pulley C and block B.
  2. Rope 1 (A round to C): \(s_A + 2s_C = l_1\).Pulley C hangs in a loop of rope 1, so \(s_C\) appears twice.
  3. Rope 2 (C round B): \(s_B + (s_B - s_C) = l_2\).One segment runs from the datum to B. The other runs from C down to B, and its length is the gap \(s_B - s_C\).
  4. Eliminate \(s_C\): doubling rope 2 gives \(4s_B - 2s_C = 2l_2\), and adding rope 1 gives \(s_A + 4s_B = l_1 + 2l_2\).
  5. Differentiate: \(v_A + 4v_B = 0 \Rightarrow v_B = -\tfrac14 (2) = -0.5\) m/s.

vB = 0.5 m/s upwards

The minus sign says B moves against the “positive down” direction, so it rises.

Method 2: the velocity line (velocity analysis)

L10 shows a much faster method that uses one fact: across a pulley, velocity changes in a straight line. Draw the pulley with a horizontal diameter, put the rope speeds as arrows at the two edges, and join their tips with a straight line. The height of that line at the centre is the speed of the pulley’s axle.

\[ v_{\text{centre}} = \frac{v_{\text{left}} + v_{\text{right}}}{2} \qquad \text{(with signs: up positive)} \]
Why the line is straight

The rope grips the pulley, so the pulley’s rim moves with the rope wherever they touch. A pulley is a rigid disc, so its velocities vary linearly across it, just like a wheel rolling along the rope. If one side of the rope is fixed, that side of the pulley is momentarily still. It is the pulley’s instantaneous centre (Chapter 8), and the speed grows in proportion to the distance from it.

  • Fixed pulley (axle fixed): the line passes through the centre, so the two rope sides move at equal speeds in opposite directions.
  • Movable pulley, one side tied: the centre moves at half the speed of the free side.
  • Both sides moving: the centre moves at the average. In L10 Example 4, A moves down at 4 m/s and C moves up at 2 m/s, so B moves at \((4-2)/2 = 1\) m/s.

Work from the pulley you know most about towards the one you want, carrying each answer across shared ropes.

In one breath
  • Rope length is constant, so differentiate the length equation.
  • A pulley in a loop of rope counts twice, which gives the factors of 2.
  • Velocity line: rope speeds at the edges, a straight line between the tips, read off the centre.
