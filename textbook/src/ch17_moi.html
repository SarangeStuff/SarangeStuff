Mass moment of inertia: rotational mass

In this chapter
  • What \(I\) measures, and why distance is squared
  • The standard shapes you need
  • Radius of gyration
  • The parallel-axis theorem and composite bodies
The big idea

Mass resists being pushed: \(F = ma\). The moment of inertia resists being spun: \(M = I\alpha\). It depends not just on how much mass there is, but on how far it sits from the axis. Mass far from the axis counts for a lot more.

\[ I = \int r^2\,dm \qquad \text{units: kg·m}^2 \]
Why r is squared

A bit of mass at radius \(r\) has to move at \(r\omega\), so it takes more effort to speed up. Its tangential acceleration \(r\alpha\) also gives it more leverage against the turning, because the moment arm is another \(r\). One \(r\) from each effect gives \(r^2\). That is why a figure skater spins faster when pulling the arms in (Chapter 15: angular momentum \(I\omega\) stays constant while \(I\) drops).

L32 says plainly that integrating to find \(I\) will no longer come up in tests or exams. You will use the table (M&K Table D/4), the parallel-axis theorem, and addition.

The shapes you need

Body (mass \(m\))Axis\(I\)
Slender rod, length \(L\)Through the centre, ⟂ rod\(\tfrac1{12}mL^2\)
Slender rod, length \(L\)Through one end, ⟂ rod\(\tfrac13mL^2\)
Thin ring or hoop, radius \(r\)Through the centre, ⟂ plane\(mr^2\)
Solid disc or cylinder, radius \(r\)Its own axis\(\tfrac12mr^2\)
Thin disc, radius \(r\)A diameter\(\tfrac14mr^2\)
Solid sphere, radius \(r\)A diameter\(\tfrac25mr^2\)
Thin rectangular plate \(a\times b\)Through the centre, ⟂ plate\(\tfrac1{12}m(a^2+b^2)\)

Sanity check: the hoop (\(mr^2\)) beats the disc (\(\tfrac12mr^2\)) because all of the hoop’s mass is at the rim.

Radius of gyration

\(I = mk^2\), so \(k = \sqrt{I/m}\). It is the radius at which you could squash all the mass into a thin ring without changing \(I\). Questions often give \(k\) instead of \(I\) (the I-beam in Chapter 19 has \(k_O = 2.32\) m).

The parallel-axis theorem

\[ I_O = I_G + m d^2 \]
Common slip

One end of the move must be the mass centre G. To go from axis O to axis P, go O → G (subtract \(md_{OG}^2\)), then G → P (add \(md_{GP}^2\)).

Worked example 17.1: a rod about a quarter point

L32 Think-Pair-Share 3

Find \(I\) for a slender rod (mass \(M\), length \(L\)) about an axis through the point \(L/4\) from one end.

  1. The axis is \(d = L/4\) from the centre, so \(I = \tfrac1{12}ML^2 + M(L/4)^2 = ML^2\left(\tfrac{4}{48} + \tfrac{3}{48}\right)\).

I = 7ML²/48

Worked example 17.2: a composite pendulum

In the style of L32 Example 1

A pendulum is a 3 kg slender rod (0.8 m), pivoted at its top end O, with a 5 kg solid disc (radius 0.1 m) centred on its bottom end. Find \(I_O\), the position of G, and \(I_G\).

  1. Rod about O (its end): \(\tfrac13(3)(0.8)^2 = 0.640\).
  2. Disc about O: its own \(\tfrac12(5)(0.1)^2 = 0.025\), plus \(5(0.8)^2 = 3.200\) from the parallel-axis theorem.The disc’s own spin barely matters. Almost all of its \(I\) comes from being far from O.
  3. \(I_O = 0.640 + 0.025 + 3.200 = 3.865\) kg·m².
  4. Mass centre: \(\bar y = \dfrac{3(0.4) + 5(0.8)}{8} = 0.650\) m below O. Then \(I_G = I_O - 8(0.65)^2\).

IO = 3.87 kg·m², G is 0.650 m below O, IG = 0.485 kg·m²

In one breath
  • \(I\) is rotational mass, and distance from the axis counts squared.
  • Know the table, and \(I = mk^2\).
  • \(I_O = I_G + md^2\), always to or from G.
  • Composites: add each part about the same axis.
