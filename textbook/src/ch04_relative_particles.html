Relative motion: what one moving observer sees

In this chapter
  • The relative-motion equation and how to read the subscripts
  • Your first vector triangle, drawn step by step
  • Relative acceleration with n–t components
The big idea

Picture yourself in car A, watching car B. What you see (B relative to A) is B’s real motion with your own motion taken away. Written the other way round, B’s real motion is your motion plus what you see. That one sentence is the equation for the whole chapter, and for Chapters 9 and 10 as well.

\[ \vec r_B = \vec r_A + \vec r_{B/A} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \vec v_B = \vec v_A + \vec v_{B/A} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \vec a_B = \vec a_A + \vec a_{B/A} \]

Read \(\vec v_{B/A}\) as “the velocity of B as seen from A”. The second and third equations are just the first one differentiated, once and twice. In L09 the observer’s axes translate but do not rotate. The observer slides along with A but keeps facing north.

Why it is just addition

Positions add like steps on a map: getting from O to B is the same as getting from O to A and then from A to B. Differentiating a sum gives the sum of the derivatives, so velocities and accelerations add the same way. Nothing more is going on.

Two habits that prevent most mistakes:

  • \(\vec v_{A/B} = -\vec v_{B/A}\). Swapping the letters flips the arrow and keeps the size.
  • The equation is one vector equation, so it is worth two scalar equations (\(x\) and \(y\)). At most two things can be unknown.

Drawing the vector triangle

The equation \(\vec v_B = \vec v_A + \vec v_{B/A}\) is a triangle. Draw \(\vec v_A\) first. Put the tail of \(\vec v_{B/A}\) at the tip of \(\vec v_A\). The line from the start to the final tip is \(\vec v_B\). Read it like a journey: go along \(\vec v_A\), then along \(\vec v_{B/A}\), and you arrive where \(\vec v_B\) takes you directly.

Worked example 4.1: two cars at a crossing

Same method as L09 Examples 1 and 3, Tut03 Q2

Car A drives east at 20 m/s. Car B drives north at 15 m/s on the crossing road. What velocity does B appear to have to the driver of A?

  1. Size, from the right angle at o: \(v_{B/A} = \sqrt{20^2 + 15^2} = 25.0\) m/s.With no right angle, you would use the cosine rule (Chapter 1).
  2. Direction: \(\tan^{-1}(15/20) = 36.9^\circ\) north of west. In the course’s angle notation that is \(\measuredangle\,143.1^\circ\).
  3. Check with components, which matches:
    \[ \vec v_{B/A} = \vec v_B - \vec v_A = (0, 15) - (20, 0) = (-20, 15)\ \text{m/s} \]

vB/A = 25.0 m/s at 36.9° north of west

It makes sense physically: to the driver of A, the whole world drifts west at 20 m/s, so B appears to move north and west.

Relative acceleration

The same triangle works for accelerations. The extra step is that a particle on a curved path has two acceleration components (Chapter 3), and you must include both.

\[ (\vec a_B)_n + (\vec a_B)_t = \vec a_A + \vec a_{B/A} \]

Worked example 4.2: the cars again, with B on a bend

Like L09 Example 3

At the same instant, A is speeding up at 2 m/s². B is on a bend of radius 100 m curving towards the west and is slowing down at 1 m/s². Find \(\vec a_{B/A}\).

  1. \(\vec a_A = 2\) m/s² east, along its straight path.
  2. \((a_B)_n = v_B^2/\rho = 15^2/100 = 2.25\) m/s², pointing into the bend (west).B is turning, so part of its acceleration must point towards the centre of the bend.
  3. \((a_B)_t = 1\) m/s² pointing south, opposite to its motion, because B is slowing down.
  4. Subtract:
    \[ \vec a_{B/A} = \vec a_B - \vec a_A = (-2.25, -1) - (2, 0) = (-4.25, -1)\ \text{m/s}^2 \]

aB/A = 4.37 m/s² at ∡ −166.8° (13.2° south of west)

In one breath
  • \(\vec v_B = \vec v_A + \vec v_{B/A}\): B’s real motion is A’s motion plus what A sees.
  • Draw the absolute vectors from one point o. The relative vector joins their tips.
  • For a curved path, split acceleration into \(n\) (\(v^2/\rho\), into the bend) and \(t\).
  • A 2D vector equation can solve for at most two unknowns.
