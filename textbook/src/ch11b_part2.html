Part II: why things move

Part I described motion without asking what causes it. Part II brings in forces and masses. The good news is that every acceleration, every \(v^2/\rho\), every \(r\ddot\theta\) and every relative acceleration diagram from Part I is reused here. Kinetics is kinematics plus one law.

\[ \sum \vec F = m\,\vec a \]

Everything else in Part II is this law in a different outfit. Integrate it along the path and you get the work–energy principle. Integrate it over time and you get impulse–momentum. Apply it to a body with size and you add a moment equation, \(\sum M_G = I_G\alpha\).

The colour code in Part II diagrams

  • Blue: a force you know, such as the weight or a given push.
  • Crimson: an unknown force, such as a reaction, tension, normal force or friction.
  • Amber: an \(m\vec a\) term on the kinetic diagram. It is the effect of the forces, not another force.
  • Green curl: an \(I_G\alpha\) term on the kinetic diagram (rigid bodies only).

Which tool? The first decision in every kinetics question

The question asks for…UseBecause
A force, tension, reaction or acceleration at one instantNewton’s second law \(\sum F = ma\) (and \(\sum M_G = I_G\alpha\))It links forces to acceleration at that moment.
A speed at one position, given the speed at another positionWork–energy \(T_1 + V_1 + U'_{1-2} = T_2 + V_2\)It is a scalar equation with no time in it. Forces that do no work drop out.
A speed after a time, or an average force during a short hitImpulse–momentum \(m\vec v_1 + \sum\int \vec F\,dt = m\vec v_2\)It deals with time and forces that act briefly.
Speeds just after a collisionConservation of momentum plus the coefficient of restitution \(e\)The impact forces are internal and huge, but they cancel in pairs.
A spin rate after something moves in or out, or an impact on a pivoted bodyAngular momentum about a fixed pointThe pivot force has no moment about the pivot.

Many exam questions chain two tools together. A typical chain is energy (falling), then momentum (impact), then energy again (compressing a spring), as in the putty question in Chapter 16. Spot where one stage ends and the next begins.
