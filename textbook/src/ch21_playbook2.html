Kinetics playbook and one-page formula sheet

The kinetics question checklist

  1. Read for the tool. Instant, force or acceleration: Newton. Speed against position: energy. Speed against time, or a short hit: impulse. Collision: momentum plus \(e\). (See the table at the start of Part II.)
  2. Split the story into stages wherever something sudden happens (an impact, a cable going slack, contact being lost). Each stage may need a different tool.
  3. Draw the FBD and the kinetic diagram for each body. Label every force. Mark the positive directions.
  4. Friction: check whether it moves, or rolls, before choosing \(\mu_s\) or \(\mu_k\).
  5. Count unknowns against equations. If you are short, the missing equation is kinematics: a rope constraint, \(a_G = \alpha r\), or a relative acceleration.
  6. Rigid bodies: take moments about the point where the most unknowns meet (a pin, or the contact point), including \(m a_G d\) if that point is not G.
  7. Answer with direction and units, for example “846 N”, “3.27 m/s² down the incline”, “14.7 rad/s² CW”.

Two memo answers to ask about

While checking every example for this book, two review-lecture memo answers did not reproduce. The methods in the memos are right. Only a number slipped.

  • Supp 2024 Q6 (rotating tube): the memo uses \(\dot\omega = -3\) where \(\dot\theta = 5\) belongs, and gets \(T = 1.64\) N. With \(\dot\theta = 5\), \(T = 7.40\) N (Chapter 13).
  • Supp Q7 (two spheres): the memo’s \(v_{A\parallel} = -1.855\) m/s gives \(e = 0.65\) in its own equation. With \(e = 0.6\), \(v_{A\parallel} = -1.707\) m/s and \(v_A = 2.85\) m/s at 53.3° (Chapter 16).

Also, from L16 in Part I: the slide uses a rod length of 0.250 m in the normal-acceleration step where the real length is 0.350 m (its answer, 305 m/s², is correct).

The whole of Part II in four sentences

Draw every force on the body, and \(m\vec a\) (plus \(I_G\alpha\)) on a separate picture. The two pictures are equal, which gives \(\sum F = ma\) and a moment equation about any point you like. Integrate that law along the path and you get energy; integrate it over time and you get momentum. Kinematics from Part I supplies whatever link between accelerations you are still missing.
