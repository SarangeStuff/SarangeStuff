MEC2047S Dynamics

Problem-Solving Playbook

Spot the topic, then run the flow. Each card tells you how to recognise the topic in a question, the steps in order, the formulas, the visual checks, and where it showed up in the 2025 papers (S25 = MEC2047S Nov 2025, F25 = MEC2047F June 2025).

Start here: the first 30 seconds

  1. Circle what is asked. A rate (ω, v, a)? A force? A time? A speed after something happens?
  2. Particle or rigid body? If the size or rotation of the object matters (links, wheels, beams, plates), it's a rigid body.
  3. Kinematics or kinetics? No masses or forces in the question → kinematics (motion only). Masses, forces, springs or friction → kinetics.
  4. Scan for trigger words in the table below to pick the topic card.
  5. Kinetics? Pick the principle with the method chooser.
  6. Draw first. The diagram earns marks and tells you every direction before you write a single equation.

Part A · Kinematics of particles

1Rectilinear motion

Spot it straight-line motion with a given as a function of t, v or x · "when x = …" · "time to come to rest" · graphs

Flow

  1. Ask: what does a depend on? t, v or x (s).
  2. Pick the relation that holds only that variable and the one you want:
    • a(t): $dv = a\,dt$, then $ds = v\,dt$
    • a(v), want t: $dt = \dfrac{dv}{a(v)}$; want x: $dx = \dfrac{v\,dv}{a(v)}$
    • a(x): $v\,dv = a(x)\,dx$
  3. Separate variables and integrate with limits (lower limits = the known starting values).
  4. An unknown constant (like k)? Use the second data point to find it first.
  5. Solve, then check signs and units.

Formulas

$v = \dfrac{ds}{dt}\qquad a = \dfrac{dv}{dt} = v\dfrac{dv}{ds}$

Constant a only:
$v = v_0 + at$
$s = s_0 + v_0t + \tfrac12at^2$
$v^2 = v_0^2 + 2a(s - s_0)$

Graphs: slope of s–t = v · slope of v–t = a · area under v–t = Δs · area under a–t = Δv

Watch-outs

  • Constant-a formulas only when a really is constant.
  • "Comes to rest" means v = 0.
  • Distance travelled ≠ displacement if v changes sign: split the integral at v = 0.

Seen in: F25 Q1 (a = −k√v)

2Normal–tangential (n–t)

Spot it a known curved path · ρ or "radius of curvature" · "speed increasing at…" · bend, hump, loop

Flow

  1. Draw the path at the point. $\vec e_t$ along the velocity (tangent); $\vec e_n$ towards the centre of curvature (inside of the bend).
  2. $a_t = \dot v$: the rate of change of speed (negative if slowing down).
  3. $a_n = v^2/\rho$: always towards the centre, never negative.
  4. Total: $a = \sqrt{a_t^2 + a_n^2}$, angle from $\tan^{-1}(a_n/a_t)$.
  5. Need x–y components? Project $a_t$ and $a_n$ using the angle the tangent makes with the horizontal.

Formulas

$\vec v = v\,\vec e_t\qquad \vec a = \dot v\,\vec e_t + \dfrac{v^2}{\rho}\vec e_n$

For a path $y = f(x)$:
$\rho = \dfrac{\left[1 + (dy/dx)^2\right]^{3/2}}{\left|d^2y/dx^2\right|}$

{{fig:nt_path}}

Watch-outs

  • km/h ÷ 3.6 = m/s.
  • ρ is the curvature of the path, not the distance to an observer (radar r ≠ ρ).
  • Constant speed still has $a_n \ne 0$.

Seen in: S25 Q1b (aircraft, a_y from a_t and a_n) · S25 Q5 (car on a bend)

3Polar coordinates (r–θ)

Spot it measured from a fixed point: radar "tracks" · r and θ given · rotating or slotted arm · collar on a rotating rod · r = f(θ)

Flow

  1. Draw the radial line from O to the particle; mark θ from the reference line (usually +x).
  2. Draw $\vec e_r$ along r, pointing away from O. Draw $\vec e_\theta$ by rotating $\vec e_r$ 90° CCW (the way θ increases).
  3. List the six numbers: $r, \dot r, \ddot r, \theta, \dot\theta, \ddot\theta$ (θ in rad). If $r = f(\theta)$, use the chain rule: $\dot r = \dfrac{dr}{d\theta}\dot\theta$, $\;\ddot r = \dfrac{d^2r}{d\theta^2}\dot\theta^2 + \dfrac{dr}{d\theta}\ddot\theta$.
  4. Plug into $v_r, v_\theta, a_r, a_\theta$.
  5. Going the other way (v or a given in x–y or n–t): project onto $\vec e_r, \vec e_\theta$, then back out $\dot r = v_r$, $\dot\theta = v_\theta/r$, $\ddot r = a_r + r\dot\theta^2$, $\ddot\theta = (a_\theta - 2\dot r\dot\theta)/r$.
  6. Signs come from the diagram: a component pointing against $\vec e_\theta$ is negative (θ decreasing = CW).

Formulas

$\vec e_r = (\cos\theta,\ \sin\theta)$
$\vec e_\theta = (-\sin\theta,\ \cos\theta)$

$v_r = \dot r\qquad v_\theta = r\dot\theta$

$a_r = \ddot r - r\dot\theta^2$
$a_\theta = r\ddot\theta + 2\dot r\dot\theta$

Projection:
$v_r = v_x\cos\theta + v_y\sin\theta$
$v_\theta = -v_x\sin\theta + v_y\cos\theta$

{{fig:polar}}
{{fig:pu_project}}

Watch-outs

  • $-r\dot\theta^2$ is the pull towards O; $2\dot r\dot\theta$ (Coriolis) is the term people forget.
  • rpm × 2π/60 = rad/s.
  • Draw the diagram with every angle: it carries marks (S25 Q1a).
  • Kinetics in polar: $\sum F_r = m a_r$, $\sum F_\theta = m a_\theta$. A slot's normal force acts along $\vec e_\theta$.

Seen in: S25 Q1a (radar and aircraft) · revision pack Tut 7 Q1 (slider in a rotating slot)

4Cylindrical and spherical coordinates

Spot it 3-D motion: helix or spiral ramp with z changing → cylindrical · radar dish with an elevation angle φ, or R, θ, φ → spherical

Flow

  1. Same as polar: draw it, then list every coordinate and its two time derivatives.
  2. Cylindrical = polar + a vertical z: add $v_z = \dot z$, $a_z = \ddot z$.
  3. Spherical: θ is measured in the horizontal plane, φ is the elevation above it. Plug into the formulas on the right.
  4. Speed $= \sqrt{\text{sum of the three components squared}}$.

Why $\cos\varphi$? When θ changes, the point moves on a horizontal circle of radius $R\cos\varphi$, so $v_\theta = (R\cos\varphi)\dot\theta$.

Formulas (φ from the horizontal)

$v_R = \dot R,\quad v_\theta = R\dot\theta\cos\varphi,\quad v_\varphi = R\dot\varphi$

$a_R = \ddot R - R\dot\varphi^2 - R\dot\theta^2\cos^2\varphi$

$a_\theta = \dfrac{\cos\varphi}{R}\dfrac{d}{dt}(R^2\dot\theta) - 2R\dot\theta\dot\varphi\sin\varphi$

$a_\varphi = \dfrac{1}{R}\dfrac{d}{dt}(R^2\dot\varphi) + R\dot\theta^2\sin\varphi\cos\varphi$

$\dfrac{d}{dt}(R^2\dot\theta) = 2R\dot R\dot\theta + R^2\ddot\theta$

{{fig:sph_geom}}

Watch-outs

  • Check whether φ is measured from the horizontal (as here) or from the vertical, which swaps sin and cos.
  • On a helix, the climb rate is linked to θ̇ through the helix angle: $\dot z = r\dot\theta\tan\gamma$.

5Constrained motion (pulleys and cables)

Spot it cables and pulleys · drum winding · blocks tied together · "drawn in at a constant rate"

Flow

  1. Choose a fixed datum (ceiling, fixed pulley axle, wall).
  2. Give every moving pulley, block or cable end a position coordinate measured from the datum. Draw them: this is a marked step.
  3. For each separate cable, write its length as the sum of its straight segments. Arcs around pulleys are constant: ignore them. A pulley hanging on n segments of one cable contributes $n\,y$.
  4. Differentiate once for velocities and again for accelerations.
  5. Convert signs to words: positive = moving away from the datum.

Formulas

$L = \sum(\text{segments}) = \text{const} \Rightarrow \sum \dot y_i = 0$

Slanted segment (S25 Q2): $\sqrt{x^2 + b^2}$
$\dfrac{d}{dt}\sqrt{x^2+b^2} = \dfrac{x\dot x}{\sqrt{x^2+b^2}}$

Cable reeled in at rate u: $\dot L = -u$

Watch-outs

  • Trace each cable with your finger and count the segments that end on each moving pulley.
  • One length equation per cable: two cables give two equations.
  • A roller moving at constant speed can still give the crate an acceleration when the rope is slanted (S25 Q2c).

Seen in: S25 Q2 (crate and roller) · F25 Q2 (hoisting drum, h = 0.3 m)

6Relative motion of particles (translating axes)

Spot it "relative to", "as seen by", "appears to" · two moving vehicles · rain, boats, rivers

Flow

  1. Write $\vec v_A = \vec v_B + \vec v_{A/B}$ ("A as seen from B" $= \vec v_A - \vec v_B$). Same form for accelerations.
  2. Draw the triangle head to tail, or work in i, j components.
  3. At most two unknowns (magnitudes or directions): solve with components, or with the sine and cosine rules.
{{fig:cars_tri}}

Watch-outs

  • $\vec v_{A/B} = -\vec v_{B/A}$: the observer is the one you subtract.
  • This simple form is only for observers that translate (don't rotate).

Part B · Kinematics of rigid bodies

7Rotation about a fixed axis

Spot it wheel, gear, drum or arm on a fixed axle · rpm · belts or meshing gears · rope unwinding from a drum

Flow

  1. Treat θ, ω, α exactly like s, v, a in card 1: $\omega = \dot\theta$, $\alpha = \dot\omega$, $\omega\,d\omega = \alpha\,d\theta$.
  2. For a point P at radius r: v = rω ⊥ OP (in the sense of rotation); $a_t = r\alpha$ ⊥ OP; $a_n = r\omega^2$ towards O.
  3. Gears or belts in contact share their contact velocity and tangential acceleration: $r_A\omega_A = r_B\omega_B$.
  4. Rope off a drum: the block has v = rω and a = rα.

Formulas

$\vec v = \vec\omega\times\vec r$
$\vec a = \vec\alpha\times\vec r - \omega^2\vec r$

Constant α: $\omega = \omega_0 + \alpha t$, $\;\omega^2 = \omega_0^2 + 2\alpha\Delta\theta$

{{fig:rot_point}}

Watch-outs

  • rpm × 2π/60 → rad/s; revolutions × 2π → rad.
  • Meshing gears turn in opposite senses; an open belt keeps the same sense.

8Relative velocity (vector and graphical)

Spot it crank, connecting rod, link, slider, four-bar · "angular velocity of link AB" · "at the instant shown"

The one idea. Two points on a rigid link can't get closer or further apart. Seen from A, point B can only move on a circle around A, so $\vec v_{B/A}$ is always ⊥ to AB, with size $\omega_{AB}\cdot AB$.

Directions you always know

  • Point on a link pinned to the ground at O: v ⊥ the radius to O, size ωr.
  • Slider: along its guide.
  • Rolling wheel: centre moves parallel to the surface at rω; contact point has v = 0.
  • Relative term: ⊥ the line joining the two points.
  • A pin shared by two links has one velocity: it's the hand-over point between links.

Vector method

  1. Write $\vec v_B = \vec v_A + \omega_{AB}\vec k\times\vec r_{B/A}$. Start from a point with a known velocity.
  2. Write $\vec r_{B/A} = (x_B - x_A)\vec i + (y_B - y_A)\vec j$ from the geometry.
  3. Cross product shortcut: $\omega\vec k\times(x\vec i + y\vec j) = \omega(-y\,\vec i + x\,\vec j)$.
  4. Equate the i and j parts: two equations, two unknowns.
  5. Assume CCW positive; a negative ω means CW.

Graphical method (velocity polygon)

  1. Draw the mechanism with all angles. Angle-chase the direction of every link.
  2. Write the equation and fill in the known/unknown table. You need exactly two unknowns.
  3. Pick a scale (e.g. 1 cm = 0.5 m/s). From the pole o, draw the fully known vector ($\vec v_A$ to a).
  4. From its tip, draw a line in the direction of $\vec v_{B/A}$ (⊥ AB), length unknown.
  5. From o, draw a line in the direction of $\vec v_B$ (slider guide, or ⊥ its pivot radius).
  6. Where the lines cross is b. Measure ob = $v_B$ and ab = $v_{B/A}$, or compute them with the sine rule.
  7. $\omega_{AB} = v_{B/A}/AB$.
  8. Sense: place the $\vec v_{B/A}$ arrow at B and see which way it turns AB about A.

Visual rules

  • "⊥ to the link" is the whole game: turn each link's direction through 90°.
  • Angles in the polygon come from the mechanism: the angle between two perpendiculars equals the angle between the original lines.
  • Head to tail: $\vec v_A$ then $\vec v_{B/A}$ lands on the tip of $\vec v_B$. Absolute velocities all start at o.
  • A point C on link AB: c divides ab in the same ratio as C divides AB (the velocity image).
  • Label the polygon (o, a, b), give the scale, and state CW/CCW in the answer.

Seen in: S25 Q4a (wheel + link, sine rule) · F25 Q4a, b (four-bar: ω_AB = 1.46 CW, ω_CB = 2.07 CCW)

9Instantaneous centre of zero velocity (IC)

Spot it "by the method of instantaneous centre" · velocity-only question about one link · rolling wheels

Flow

  1. On the link, take two points whose velocity directions you know (A and B).
  2. Through each, draw a line ⊥ to its velocity. Where they cross is the IC (C).
    • Rolling without slipping: IC = contact point.
    • Parallel velocities: use the magnitudes (similar triangles). Parallel and equal: translation, ω = 0.
  3. Geometry: find $r_{A/C}$ and $r_{B/C}$ (trig, sine or cosine rule).
  4. $\omega = v_A/r_{A/C}$, then $v_B = \omega\,r_{B/C}$ (⊥ CB). Any point: $v_P = \omega\,r_{P/C}$.
  5. Sense: the link turns about C as if it were pinned there.
{{fig:ic_ladder}}

Watch-outs

  • Velocities only. The IC usually has an acceleration, so never use it for accelerations.
  • Each link has its own IC, and it moves from instant to instant.

Seen in: F25 Q3 (IC of AB, v_B = 0.270 m/s) · S25 Q4a (wheel contact point as IC)

10Relative acceleration (vector and graphical)

Spot it "angular acceleration of AB" · "acceleration of the slider/piston" · "accelerating at this instant" · "scaled vector diagram"

Always do velocities first. Every normal term needs the ω of its link.

$\vec a_B = \vec a_A + (\vec a_{B/A})_n + (\vec a_{B/A})_t$

  • $(a_{B/A})_n = \omega_{AB}^2\,AB$, pointing from B towards A along the link. Known once ω is known.
  • $(a_{B/A})_t = \alpha_{AB}\,AB$, ⊥ AB. Usually the unknown.

Directions you always know

  • Point pinned to the ground: $a_n = \omega^2 r$ towards the pivot, $a_t = \alpha r$ ⊥ the radius.
  • Slider: along its guide.
  • Rolling wheel: centre $a_O = r\alpha$ along the surface; contact point $a = r\omega^2$ towards the centre (not zero!).

Vector method

$\vec a_B = \vec a_A + \alpha\vec k\times\vec r_{B/A} - \omega^2\vec r_{B/A}$; equate the i and j parts.

{{fig:acc_concept}}

Graphical method (acceleration polygon)

  1. Compute every normal component from your velocity answers.
  2. Write the full equation, e.g. $(\vec a_B)_n + (\vec a_B)_t = \vec a_A + (\vec a_{B/A})_n + (\vec a_{B/A})_t$. The table should leave two unknown sizes (usually the two tangential terms).
  3. Pick a scale. From o, draw the known right-hand terms head to tail: $\vec a_A$, then $(\vec a_{B/A})_n$ pointing B→A.
  4. Through that tip, draw a line ⊥ AB for $(\vec a_{B/A})_t$.
  5. From o, draw the known left-hand term, $(\vec a_B)_n$ pointing B→C, then a line ⊥ CB through its tip.
  6. The crossing is b. Measure both tangential components; $\alpha = a_t/\text{length}$.
  7. o→b is the absolute $\vec a_B$.
  8. Sense of α: place the tangential arrow at the point and see which way it turns the link.

Visual rules and traps

  • Normal arrows lie along links and point towards the reference point or pivot; tangential arrows are ⊥ to links.
  • Don't drop $\vec a_A$ when the reference point accelerates (F25 Q4: $a_A$ = 0.25 m/s² ←).
  • From the velocity polygon, $a_n = v_{rel}^2/L$ gives the same number as $\omega^2 L$.
  • Diagram marks: the direction of each tangential component plus correct proportion. Memos accept a ±5 % range.
  • "Irrespective of your answer to (a), use ω = …" → use the given values.

Seen in: S25 Q4b (wheel + link, scaled diagram) · F25 Q4c, d (α_AB = 2.01 CW, α_CB = 3.81 CCW)

11Rolling without slipping (kinematics)

Spot it "rolls without slipping" · wheel on the ground · gear on a fixed rack · cable unwinding from a spool

Facts to use

  • Centre: $v_O = r\omega$, $a_O = r\alpha$, both parallel to the surface.
  • Contact point C is the IC: $v_C = 0$, but $a_C = r\omega^2$ towards O.
  • Top point: $v = 2r\omega$. Any point P: $v_P = \omega\cdot CP$, ⊥ CP.
  • Spool with a cable: the cable speed = ω × (distance from the contact point to the cable line).
  • Clockwise rolling on the ground → the wheel moves to the right.

Seen in: S25 Q4 (wheel rolling clockwise, joined to link AB)

12Geometry, then differentiate (absolute motion)

Spot it "Hint: the cosine rule will be useful" · lengths and angles tied by a fixed geometry · find θ̇ from a given φ̇

  1. Write one geometric relation linking the variables: sine rule, cosine rule, Pythagoras, or $x = L\cos\theta$.
  2. Differentiate with respect to time using the chain rule: $\dfrac{d}{dt}\sin\theta = \cos\theta\,\dot\theta$ and $\dfrac{d}{dt}\cos\theta = -\sin\theta\,\dot\theta$.
  3. Substitute the values at the instant and solve for the unknown rate. Differentiate again for accelerations.

Watch-outs

  • Link lengths are constants; angles and the distances between moving points are variables.
  • Work in radians. Keep the given sense (CW or CCW) as a sign.

Seen in: S25 Q3 (crank CB and rod AB, θ̇ = 0 at the instant)

Part C · Kinetics of particles

13Newton's second law (ΣF = ma)

Spot it a force and an acceleration at one instant: tension, normal reaction, friction · banked track · "maximum speed before slipping" · "loses contact"

Flow

  1. FBD: isolate the particle and draw every force: weight, normals, friction, tensions, springs.
  2. KD: draw $m\vec a$ in your chosen components.
  3. Choose axes that match the motion: along/⊥ an incline (rectilinear), $m v^2/\rho$ towards the centre (n–t), or $m a_r$ and $m a_\theta$ (polar).
  4. Write ΣF = ma in each direction, fill in the a's from kinematics, and solve.
  5. Friction: no slip → F ≤ μsN (unknown, check it); slipping → F = μkN, opposing the relative motion.
{{fig:k_bank_kd}}

Watch-outs

  • On a banked bend, N ≠ mg. Take n horizontal, towards the centre of the bend (S25 Q5).
  • Unsure of friction's direction? Assume one; a negative answer flips it.
  • Connected bodies: a separate FBD for each, plus the cable constraint (card 5).
  • "Loses contact" means N = 0.

Seen in: S25 Q5 (F1 car on a 10° bank, F_f = 1437 N down the slope)

14Work–energy (particles)

Spot it speed after moving x or falling h · spring compressed or stretched · "released from rest" · maximum compression · speed at a position

Flow

  1. Define states 1 and 2 and sketch both. Pick a datum for gravity.
  2. $T = \tfrac12 mv^2$ in each state.
  3. $V_g = mgh$ (h above the datum). $V_e = \tfrac12 kx^2$, with x measured from the spring's natural length.
  4. $U'_{1\text{-}2}$ = work by every other force: friction $-\mu_k N d$, applied forces $F d$. Normal forces ⊥ to the motion do no work.
  5. $T_1 + V_1 + U'_{1\text{-}2} = T_2 + V_2$, then solve.

Formulas

$U = \displaystyle\int \vec F\cdot d\vec r$

Power: $P = \vec F\cdot\vec v$
Efficiency: $e = P_{out}/P_{in}$

Non-linear spring: $V_e = \displaystyle\int F\,dx$

Watch-outs

  • Vertical spring resting at equilibrium: first find $\delta_{st} = mg/k$, then the real stretch in each state (F25 Q7).
  • Shortcut: measure x from equilibrium and drop gravity: $\tfrac12 m v^2 = \tfrac12 k(x_1^2 - x_2^2)$.
  • Energy gives a speed, not a direction or a time.

Seen in: F25 Q7 (plunger, v = 1.119 m/s)

15Impulse–momentum (particles)

Spot it "after t seconds" · force applied for a time · F(t) graph · impulse · average force during contact

  1. Choose positive directions. Draw the impulse–momentum diagram: $m\vec v_1 + \sum\int\vec F\,dt = m\vec v_2$.
  2. $\int F\,dt$ = area under the F–t graph. A constant force gives $F\,\Delta t$; include the weight × t and normal forces if they act.
  3. Solve each direction separately. With no external impulse in a direction, momentum is conserved in that direction.

Watch-outs

  • It's a vector equation: keep the signs per direction.
  • During a short impact, ignore non-impulsive forces like the weight.

16Impact (direct and oblique)

Spot it "collide", "strikes", "rebounds" · coefficient of restitution e

Flow

  1. Draw the line of impact n through the two centres (or ⊥ the contact surface), with t ⊥ n.
  2. Resolve every initial velocity into n and t components with consistent signs.
  3. t: on smooth contact, each body keeps its own t-velocity.
  4. n, momentum: $m_A(v_A)_n + m_B(v_B)_n = m_A(v'_A)_n + m_B(v'_B)_n$.
  5. n, restitution: $e = \dfrac{(v'_B)_n - (v'_A)_n}{(v_A)_n - (v_B)_n}$ (separation ÷ approach).
  6. Solve the two equations, then recombine: speed $= \sqrt{n^2 + t^2}$, angle from tan⁻¹.

Special cases

  • Constrained body (a bat in a slot, F25 Q8): the guide supplies an impulse, so conserve momentum only along the free direction. Restitution still acts along n.
  • Fixed wall: $(v')_n = -e\,v_n$, and t is unchanged.
  • e = 1 is elastic; e = 0 means they stick together.
{{fig:k_oblique}}

Watch-outs

  • Kinetic energy is lost unless e = 1.
  • "Speeds" means magnitudes only (S25 Q6).

Seen in: S25 Q6 (two disks, 5-12-13 line) · F25 Q8 (bat and ball, v′ = 30.0 m/s at 45.7°)

17Angular momentum of particles

Spot it string through a hole · central force · a moment about O that is zero or given

  1. $H_O = m v\,d$, where d is the ⊥ distance from O to the velocity line. Equivalently $H_O = m r v_\theta = m r^2\dot\theta$.
  2. $\sum M_O = \dot H_O$. If $\sum M_O = 0$ (central force), $H_O$ is conserved: $r_1 v_{\theta 1} = r_2 v_{\theta 2}$.
  3. Pair it with energy when the speed also changes.

19Rigid-body equations of motion

Spot it "angular acceleration of the body" · "reaction at the pin" · "tension in the cable" · "immediately after" · cable breaks / support removed / released

Classify the motion first

  • Translation (α = 0): $\sum\vec F = m\vec a_G$, $\sum M_G = 0$.
  • Fixed axis about O: $\sum M_O = I_O\alpha$, with $(a_G)_t = \bar r\alpha$ and $(a_G)_n = \bar r\omega^2$ towards O.
  • General plane motion: $\sum\vec F = m\vec a_G$ and $\sum M_G = I_G\alpha$, or about any point P: $\sum M_P = I_G\alpha + (\text{moment of } m\vec a_G \text{ about P})$.

Flow

  1. Draw the FBD (including pin reactions $O_x$, $O_y$) and the KD ($m\bar a_x$, $m\bar a_y$ or n–t parts, plus $I_G\alpha$).
  2. Take moments about a pin so the unknown reactions drop out.
  3. Kinematics: tie $\vec a_G$ to α (fixed axis, relative acceleration, or rolling).
  4. Count: 3 equations plus the kinematic links must match the unknowns.
{{fig:k_rod_fbd}} {{fig:k_rod_kd}}

"Suddenly released" special case

  • At that instant ω = 0: every normal acceleration is zero and only the α terms remain (F25 Q6, Q9).
  • A cable end that's still attached accelerates ⊥ to its cable.
  • $\sum M_O = I_O\alpha$ is valid only about a fixed axis O or about G.

Seen in: S25 Q7b (pin reaction F_O = 3.58 N) · F25 Q6 (T = 392 N) · F25 Q9 (α = 3.19 rad/s²)

20Rolling bodies (kinetics)

Spot it disk, cylinder or wheel on a surface with friction · "rolls" · "does it slip?" · minimum μ

Flow

  1. FBD: W, N, friction F at the contact (assume a direction), plus applied forces.
  2. Assume no slip: $a_G = r\alpha$.
  3. $\sum F_{\parallel} = m a_G$; $\sum F_{\perp} = 0$ gives N; $\sum M_G = I_G\alpha$.
  4. Solve for F, then check $|F| \le \mu_s N$.
  5. If the check fails, it slips: set $F = \mu_k N$, and $a_G$ and α become independent.
{{fig:k_roll_fbd}}

Watch-outs

  • Friction does no work when rolling without slip, so energy methods still work.
  • Friction's direction is not always obvious: assume it, and let the sign tell you.

21Rigid-body work–energy

Spot it "angular velocity when θ = 90°" · released from rest · rotates through an angle · spring attached · speed after rolling a distance

Flow

  1. States 1 and 2; datum for gravity at a convenient level.
  2. T for every moving body. Use the kinematics to write all of them in one unknown (v_G = rω, etc.).
  3. $V_g = m g h_G$, using the height of the mass centre.
  4. $V_e = \tfrac12 kx^2$, with x from the natural length. Use geometry (sine rule) for the spring length in each state (S25 Q8).
  5. $T_1 + V_1 + U'_{1\text{-}2} = T_2 + V_2$, then solve.

Kinetic energy

General: $T = \tfrac12 m v_G^2 + \tfrac12 I_G\omega^2$

Fixed axis O: $T = \tfrac12 I_O\omega^2$

Rolling, no slip: $T = \tfrac12 I_C\omega^2$ (C = contact point)

Seen in: S25 Q8 (square plate and spring, ω = 6.95 rad/s)

22Rigid-body impulse–momentum

Spot it "angular velocity after t s" · drum or flywheel · torque or tension applied for a time

  1. Linear: $m\vec v_{G1} + \sum\int\vec F\,dt = m\vec v_{G2}$.
  2. Angular: $I_G\omega_1 + \sum\int M_G\,dt = I_G\omega_2$ (or about a fixed axis O with $I_O$).
  3. No external moment about an axis → angular momentum about it is conserved (e.g. about a pin during an impact).

Watch-outs

  • Moments about G are valid even when G accelerates (F25 Q5: the drum rides on a moving carriage).
  • Ignore the distractors: the carriage's speed doesn't change the drum's spin.
  • Keep one sign convention (CCW +) all the way through.

Seen in: F25 Q5 (drum, ω = 7.85 rad/s CW)

Geometry toolkit and exam habits

⚙Geometry and maths you'll use everywhere

  • Sine rule: $\dfrac{a}{\sin A} = \dfrac{b}{\sin B} = \dfrac{c}{\sin C}$. Use it when you know two angles and a side (velocity triangles).
  • Cosine rule: $c^2 = a^2 + b^2 - 2ab\cos C$. Use it for two sides and the angle between them.
  • Angle sum: the angles of a triangle add to 180°.
  • Perpendicular rule: the angle between two lines equals the angle between their perpendiculars.
  • Projection: component = size × cos(angle to that axis).
  • Cross product: $\vec k\times\vec i = \vec j$, $\;\vec k\times\vec j = -\vec i$.
  • Chain rule: $\dfrac{d}{dt}f(\theta) = f'(\theta)\,\dot\theta$.

km/h ÷ 3.6 → m/s · rpm × 2π/60 → rad/s · rev × 2π → rad · mm → m · g → kg · kN/m × 1000 → N/m

✓Exam habits that collect marks

  1. Draw the diagram first: FBD, KD, datum, coordinates, e_r and e_θ, or n and t. Diagrams carry marks.
  2. State your positive directions and assumptions (no slip, smooth contact, slender rod).
  3. Write the general equation before the numbers: that's the method mark.
  4. Answers need units and a direction: CW/CCW, an angle, or ↑ ↓ ← →.
  5. Sanity-check signs and sizes. A cable can't push. A speed can't be negative.
  6. Stuck on part (a)? Use the values the paper gives for part (b) and keep going.
  7. Graphical answers: label the points (o, a, b), state the scale, and measure carefully. Small differences are accepted.
  8. Keep 3–4 significant figures through the working; round only the final answer.
