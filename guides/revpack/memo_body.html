Polar toolkit (read this first)

Use polar coordinates whenever something is measured as a distance r from a fixed point O and an angle θ: a rotating slotted arm, a radar, a spiral guide. In this pack that means Tutorial 2 Q3–Q5 and Tutorial 7 Q1.

1. Draw the two unit vectors

\[ \begin{gathered} \hat e_r = \cos\theta\,\hat i + \sin\theta\,\hat j \\[4pt] \hat e_\theta = -\sin\theta\,\hat i + \cos\theta\,\hat j \end{gathered} \]

Why the minus: \(\hat e_\theta\) is \(\hat e_r\) turned 90°, so it points at angle θ + 90°. Since \(\cos(\theta + 90^\circ) = -\sin\theta\), its horizontal part points backwards (left).

2. They turn as P moves

3. Velocity and acceleration (differentiate \(\vec r = r\,\hat e_r\))

\[ \begin{gathered} \vec v = \dot r\,\hat e_r + r\dot\theta\,\hat e_\theta \\[4pt] \vec a = \big(\ddot r - r\dot\theta^2\big)\,\hat e_r + \big(r\ddot\theta + 2\dot r\dot\theta\big)\,\hat e_\theta \end{gathered} \]
TermMeaning
\(\dot r\)Sliding along the arm (towards or away from O).
\(r\dot\theta\)Being carried round by the arm (radius × angular speed).
\(\ddot r\)The slide speeding up or slowing down.
\(-r\dot\theta^2\)Centripetal: anything carried round O must accelerate towards O.
\(r\ddot\theta\)The arm’s spin speeding up or slowing down.
\(2\dot r\dot\theta\)Coriolis: sliding outward on a spinning arm. One \(\dot r\dot\theta\) comes from \(\hat e_r\) turning, the other from \(r\dot\theta\) growing.

4. Switching from x–y to r–θ (dot product with each unit vector)

\[ \begin{gathered} v_r = \vec v\cdot\hat e_r = v_x\cos\theta + v_y\sin\theta \\[4pt] v_\theta = \vec v\cdot\hat e_\theta = -v_x\sin\theta + v_y\cos\theta \end{gathered} \]

The same works for \(\vec a\), and for forces: to get a force’s r and θ parts, dot it with \(\hat e_r\) and \(\hat e_\theta\). That is how gravity and cable tensions are resolved in Tut 7 Q1.

5. Polar kinetics (Newton’s second law in r–θ)

\[ \begin{gathered} \sum F_r = m\big(\ddot r - r\dot\theta^2\big) \\[4pt] \sum F_\theta = m\big(r\ddot\theta + 2\dot r\dot\theta\big) \end{gathered} \]

6. The method: fill in six numbers

valueraterate of rate
distance\(r\)\(\dot r\)\(\ddot r\)
angle\(\theta\)\(\dot\theta\)\(\ddot\theta\)
  • \(r(t)\), \(\theta(t)\) given: differentiate twice (Tut 2 Q3).
  • Path \(r = f(\theta)\): \(\dot r = f'(\theta)\dot\theta\) and \(\ddot r = f''(\theta)\dot\theta^2 + f'(\theta)\ddot\theta\) (Tut 2 Q4).
  • Geometry with a constant length (a cord, a rod): write the length in terms of r and θ, then differentiate the whole equation twice (Tut 7 Q1).
  • x–y motion known: project it onto \(\hat e_r\) and \(\hat e_\theta\) (Tut 2 Q5).
Polar traps
  • Angles in radians. deg/s × π/180 = rad/s.
  • Don’t mix up \(\dot\theta\) and \(\ddot\theta\) when substituting.
  • Signs mean directions: negative \(\dot r\) means moving towards O.
  • If θ is measured from a different line (for example the vertical), \(\hat e_\theta\) still points the way that θ increases.

Tut 0 Q1: Jill chasing the shopping cart

Memo answer: the cart rolls 6.7 m.

  1. Cart’s acceleration: on a frictionless 3° slope, \(a_C = g\sin3^\circ = 0.513\) m/s².Gravity points straight down. Only its component along the slope, \(g\sin\theta\), can speed the cart up.
  2. Positions, measured from Jill’s start (both start from rest at \(t = 0\)): \(x_J = \tfrac12(2.0)t^2\) and \(x_C = 20 + \tfrac12(0.513)t^2\).
  3. She catches it when \(x_J = x_C\): \(t^2 = \dfrac{20}{1 - 0.2565}\), so \(t = 5.19\) s.“Catches” means both are at the same place at the same time.
  4. Distance the cart rolled: \(\tfrac12(0.513)(5.19)^2 = 6.9\) m.

The cart rolls about 6.9 m (the memo gets 6.7 m because it rounds aC to 0.5 m/s²)

Tut 0 Q2: the arrow and the ball

Memo answers: (a) 30.3 m/s, (b) 2.09 s after the arrow.

  1. When does the arrow reach the assistant? Horizontally the speed is constant: \(150 = 45\cos50^\circ\,t\), so \(t_1 = 5.19\) s.No horizontal force, so constant horizontal speed. Use x to find the time.
  2. How high is it then? \(y = 45\sin50^\circ(5.19) - 4.905(5.19)^2 = 46.9\) m.
  3. (a) Minimum throw speed: the ball must just reach 46.9 m, so it arrives there with zero speed: \(0 = v_0^2 - 2(9.81)(46.9)\), giving \(v_0 = 30.3\) m/s.Any slower and it never gets that high. At exactly this speed the arrow meets the ball at the top of its flight.
  4. (b) When to throw: going up to the top takes \(v_0/g = 3.09\) s, so throw at \(5.19 - 3.09 = 2.09\) s after the arrow.

(a) 30.3 m/s, (b) 2.09 s after the arrow is launched

Tut 0 Q3: the swing ride

Memo answers: (a) 10 m/s², (b) 47°.

  1. Units first: 25.0 mph × 0.447 = 11.18 m/s.
  2. (a) \(a = v^2/r = 11.18^2/12 = 10.4\) m/s², towards the axis.
  3. (b) FBD of the rider: the wire tension T at angle θ from the vertical, and the weight mg. Vertical (no vertical acceleration): \(T\cos\theta = mg\). Horizontal (towards the centre): \(T\sin\theta = mv^2/r\).The tension is the only force with a horizontal part, so it must supply the centripetal force.
  4. Divide the two equations: \(\tan\theta = \dfrac{v^2}{rg} = \dfrac{10.4}{9.81}\), so θ = 46.7°.

(a) 10.4 m/s² (the memo rounds to 10), (b) θ = 47°

Tut 0 Q4: two blocks over a pulley with mass

Memo answer: \(a = \dfrac{m_2g}{m_1 + m_2 + \tfrac12m_p}\).

  1. Block 1 (frictionless table): \(T_1 = m_1a\).
  2. Block 2 (hanging, down positive): \(m_2g - T_2 = m_2a\).
  3. Pulley: \((T_2 - T_1)R = I\alpha = \tfrac12m_pR^2\,\dfrac{a}{R}\), so \(T_2 - T_1 = \tfrac12m_pa\).A pulley with mass needs a net torque to spin up, so the two tensions must differ. The string doesn’t slip, so \(a = \alpha R\).
  4. Add all three equations so the tensions cancel: \(m_2g = (m_1 + m_2 + \tfrac12m_p)a\).

a = m₂g / (m₁ + m₂ + ½mp)

The pulley acts like an extra mass of \(\tfrac12m_p\) that has to be accelerated.

Tut 1 Q1: from the v–t triangle to s–t and a–t

Memo: s = 50t² up to 0.1 s (reaching 0.5 m), then s = −50t² + 20t − 1 (reaching 1.0 m). a = +100 then −100 m/s².

  1. Read the v–t graph: v rises from 0 to 10 m/s in 0.1 s, so \(v = 100t\). Then it falls back to 0 at 0.2 s, so \(v = -100t + 20\).
  2. a–t: the slope of each straight piece, +100 then −100 m/s².Acceleration is the slope of v–t.
  3. s–t: integrate each piece. \(s = 50t^2\) gives 0.5 m at 0.1 s, which matches “s = 0.5 m at t′/2”. Starting from there, \(s = -50t^2 + 20t - 1\) gives 1.0 m at 0.2 s.Displacement is the area under v–t. Each piece starts where the last one ended, so the curve never jumps.
  4. Shape: s–t curves upward (getting steeper) until 0.1 s, then curves over and is flat at 0.2 s, where v = 0.

Tut 1 Q2: from the a–s graph to the v–s graph

Memo: v = 0.1s up to 200 m (20 m/s), then v = √(−0.02s² + 12s − 1200), reaching 24.5 m/s at 300 m.

  1. Write a(s) for each piece: \(a = 0.01s\) for 0–200 m, and \(a = -0.02s + 6\) for 200–300 m.
  2. Use \(v\,dv = a\,ds\), because a is given against s, not t: \(\int_0^v v\,dv = \int_0^s 0.01s\,ds\) gives \(v = 0.1s\), so 20 m/s at 200 m.
  3. Second piece: \(\int_{20}^{v} v\,dv = \int_{200}^{s}(-0.02s + 6)\,ds\) gives \(v^2 = -0.02s^2 + 12s - 1200\), so \(v(300) = 24.5\) m/s.
  4. Shape: a straight line up to 200 m, then a curve that flattens to a horizontal tangent at 300 m (where a = 0).\(dv/ds = a/v\), so where a = 0 the v–s curve is flat.

Tut 1 Q3: ball sinking in a lake, a = 10 − 0.8v

Memo answers: depth 28.4 m, speed 11.8 m/s.

  1. a depends on v, and time is given, so use \(a = dv/dt\):
    \[ \int_{4.5}^{v}\frac{dv}{10 - 0.8v} = \int_0^t dt \]
  2. Integrate and rearrange: \(v = 12.5 - 8e^{-0.8t}\).12.5 m/s is the terminal speed, where \(10 - 0.8v = 0\). The ball approaches it but never reaches it.
  3. Depth: \(x = \int_0^3 v\,dt = 12.5t + 10e^{-0.8t} - 10\), which is 28.4 m at 3 s.
  4. Speed at the bottom: \(v(3) = 11.8\) m/s.

Depth 28.4 m, v = 11.8 m/s

Tut 1 Q4: rocket sled and water brake

Memo answers: 2300 m, 12.5 s (the memo writes “12.5 m” for the time, a typo).

  1. Phase 1, a(t) = 30 + 2t: integrate to get \(v = 30t + t^2\) and \(s = 15t^2 + t^3/3\). It reaches 400 m/s at \(t = 10\) s, after \(s_1 = 1833\) m.
  2. Phase 2, a(v) = −0.003v², distance: use \(v\,dv = a\,ds\), so \(ds = -\dfrac{dv}{0.003v}\) and \(\Delta s = \dfrac{1}{0.003}\ln\dfrac{400}{100} = 462\) m.
  3. Phase 2, time: use \(dt = dv/a\): \(\Delta t = \dfrac{1}{0.003}\left(\dfrac1{100} - \dfrac1{400}\right) = 2.5\) s.The same a(v) gives distance through \(v\,dv = a\,ds\) and time through \(dt = dv/a\). Pick whichever the question asks for.
  4. Totals: \(1833 + 462 = 2295 \approx 2300\) m, and \(10 + 2.5 = 12.5\) s.

Tut 1 Q5: air from a vent, v = 0.18v₀/x

Memo answers: (a) −0.0525 m/s², (b) 6.17 s.

  1. \(v = 0.648/x\) (with \(v_0 = 3.6\)).
  2. (a) v is known as a function of x, so \(a = v\,\dfrac{dv}{dx} = \dfrac{0.648}{x}\cdot\left(-\dfrac{0.648}{x^2}\right) = -\dfrac{0.420}{x^3}\). At x = 2: −0.0525 m/s².\(a = dv/dt = (dv/dx)(dx/dt) = v\,dv/dx\).
  3. (b) Time: \(dt = dx/v = \dfrac{x\,dx}{0.648}\), so \(t = \dfrac{3^2 - 1^2}{2(0.648)} = 6.17\) s.

Tut 2 Q1: package dropped from a diving aircraft

Answer on the sheet: d = 99.6 m.

  1. The package starts with the plane’s velocity: 200 m/s at 30° from the vertical, so \(v_x = 200\sin30^\circ = 100\) m/s and \(v_y = -200\cos30^\circ = -173.2\) m/s (downwards).When you let go of something it keeps the velocity it had. Only gravity changes it after that.
  2. Time to fall 1200 m: \(1200 = 173.2t + 4.905t^2\), so \(t = 5.93\) s (the positive root).
  3. Horizontal travel: \(100 \times 5.93 = 593\) m.
  4. Where the target is: the plane is diving straight at A, so A lies on the flight line. Its horizontal distance is \(1200\tan30^\circ = 692.8\) m.
  5. The miss: \(d = 692.8 - 593.2\).Gravity curves the package’s path down below the straight flight line, so it lands short.

d = 99.6 m short of the target

Tut 2 Q2: jet on the parabola y = 0.0004x²

Answer on the sheet: a = 0.921 m/s².

  1. Locate A from the figure: x = 5 km, y = 0.0004(5000)² = 10 km.
  2. Derivatives: \(y' = 0.0008x = 4\) and \(y'' = 0.0008\).
  3. Radius of curvature: \(\rho = \dfrac{(1 + 4^2)^{3/2}}{0.0008} = 87\,600\) m.The hint formula measures how sharply the path bends at A. Steep parts of a parabola bend gently, so ρ is large.
  4. Components: \(a_t = 0.80\) m/s² (given), and \(a_n = 200^2/87\,616 = 0.457\) m/s².
  5. Combine: they are perpendicular, so \(a = \sqrt{0.8^2 + 0.457^2}\).

Tut 2 Q3 (polar): oscillating rod with a sliding collar

Answers on the sheet: 15.6 m/s, 50.8 m/s², −3.25 er m/s².

  1. Draw it: the rod hangs from O, and θ is measured from the vertical. \(\hat e_r\) points along the rod, away from O (down the rod). \(\hat e_\theta\) is perpendicular to it, pointing the way θ opens.Polar coordinates don’t care which line θ is measured from. \(\hat e_\theta\) always points the way θ increases.
  2. The six numbers. \(\theta = \tfrac{3}{\pi}\sin(\pi t)\), so \(\dot\theta = 3\cos(\pi t)\) and \(\ddot\theta = -3\pi\sin(\pi t)\). \(r = 6(1 - e^{-2t})\), so \(\dot r = 12e^{-2t}\) and \(\ddot r = -24e^{-2t}\).
  3. At t = 1 s: \(\theta = 0\), \(\dot\theta = -3\) rad/s, \(\ddot\theta = 0\); \(r = 5.188\) m, \(\dot r = 1.624\) m/s, \(\ddot r = -3.248\) m/s².\(\sin\pi = 0\) and \(\cos\pi = -1\). At this instant the rod passes the vertical at full swing speed.
  4. Speed: \(v_r = 1.624\) and \(v_\theta = r\dot\theta = 5.188(-3) = -15.56\), so \(v = \sqrt{1.624^2 + 15.56^2} = 15.6\) m/s.
  5. Acceleration: \(a_r = -3.248 - 5.188(9) = -49.9\) and \(a_\theta = 5.188(0) + 2(1.624)(-3) = -9.74\), so \(a = 50.9\) m/s².The big \(-r\dot\theta^2\) term dominates: the collar is being swung round fast at a large radius.
  6. Relative to the rod: someone on the rod only sees the sliding, \(\ddot r\,\hat e_r = -3.25\,\hat e_r\) m/s² (3.25 m/s² towards O).

v = 15.6 m/s, a = 50.9 m/s², arel = −3.25 er m/s²

Tut 2 Q5 (polar): radar tracking a plane

Answers on the sheet: r = 3000 m, ṙ = 370 m/s, r̈ = 57.9 m/s², θ = 36.9°, θ̇ = −0.0924 rad/s, θ̈ = 0.0315 rad/s².

Memo answer: μk = 0.349.

Spherical coordinates primer

There are no spherical questions in this pack, but L08 examines them. Use spherical coordinates for anything pointing into the sky from a fixed point, like a radar antenna or a fire-truck ladder: \(R\) is the distance along the beam, \(\theta\) is the compass angle in the horizontal plane, and \(\phi\) is the elevation above horizontal.

\[ \vec v = \dot R\,\hat e_R + R\dot\theta\cos\phi\,\hat e_\theta + R\dot\phi\,\hat e_\phi \]

Why \(\cos\phi\): changing θ swings the point round a horizontal circle whose radius is its horizontal distance from the axis, \(R\cos\phi\). Changing φ swings it round a vertical circle of radius R.

\[ \begin{gathered} a_R = \ddot R - R\dot\phi^2 - R\dot\theta^2\cos^2\phi \\[4pt] a_\theta = 2\dot R\dot\theta\cos\phi + R\ddot\theta\cos\phi - 2R\dot\theta\dot\phi\sin\phi \\[4pt] a_\phi = 2\dot R\dot\phi + R\ddot\phi + R\dot\theta^2\sin\phi\cos\phi \end{gathered} \]
How to rebuild these from polar

Vertical plane (R, φ): this is polar with r → R and θ → φ. It gives \(\ddot R - R\dot\phi^2\) and \(R\ddot\phi + 2\dot R\dot\phi\).

Horizontal circle (radius ρ = R cos φ): this is also polar. It gives \(a_\theta = \rho\ddot\theta + 2\dot\rho\dot\theta\), and a centripetal \(\rho\dot\theta^2\) towards the axis. That centripetal part splits into \(-R\dot\theta^2\cos^2\phi\) in \(a_R\) and \(+R\dot\theta^2\sin\phi\cos\phi\) in \(a_\phi\).

Method: fill in nine numbers (\(R, \dot R, \ddot R, \theta, \dot\theta, \ddot\theta, \phi, \dot\phi, \ddot\phi\)) in radians, then substitute. To turn cylindrical components into spherical ones, rotate by φ: \(u_R = u_r\cos\phi + u_z\sin\phi\), \(u_\phi = -u_r\sin\phi + u_z\cos\phi\), and \(u_\theta\) stays the same.

Quick check (L08 fire-truck ladder): R = 15 m, Ṙ = 0.5 m/s, θ̇ = 10°/s, φ = 30°, φ̇ = 7°/s give v = 2.96 m/s and a = 0.672 m/s². The full working is in the Polar & 3D guide, Section 14.
