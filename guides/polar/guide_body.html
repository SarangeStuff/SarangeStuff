1. Why polar coordinates?

In x–y coordinates you say where a point is by walking along \(x\) and then along \(y\). In polar coordinates you say where it is by how far it is from a fixed point O (the distance \(r\)) and which way you look to see it (the angle \(\theta\)). That is exactly how many machines and sensors work:

  • a rotating arm or slotted link with something sliding along it (the arm sets \(\theta\), the slider sets \(r\));
  • a radar that measures distance and bearing;
  • a robot arm that extends and swings;
  • a path described as \(r = f(\theta)\) (spiral, cardioid, circle through O).
How to spot a polar question

Look for a rotating line through a fixed point, with something moving along it, or a question that gives or asks for \(r, \dot r, \ddot r, \theta, \dot\theta, \ddot\theta\). If you can name a fixed point O and a line from O to the particle that the problem cares about, use polar.

The one new idea compared with x–y: the direction arrows (unit vectors) are not fixed. They point along and across the line OP, so they turn as the particle moves. Every extra term in the polar formulas comes from that turning. Once you understand Section 4, the formulas stop being things to memorise and become things you can rebuild in a minute.

2. The two unit vectors: how to draw them

At the particle P, draw two arrows, each of length 1:

  1. Draw the radial line: a straight line from O through P, and a little beyond.
  2. \(\hat e_r\) starts at P and points along the radial line, away from O.“r” grows in this direction. Moving along \(\hat e_r\) increases the distance from O.
  3. \(\hat e_\theta\) starts at P, is perpendicular to the radial line, and points the way \(\theta\) increases.“θ” grows in this direction. If \(\theta\) is measured anticlockwise, \(\hat e_\theta\) points anticlockwise around O.
  4. Mark the right angle between them.
When θ is measured some other way

The rule is always “\(\hat e_\theta\) points the way θ increases”. If a figure measures θ from the vertical, or clockwise (Tut02 Q3 measures θ from a hanging rod), then \(\hat e_\theta\) points the way that angle opens. Draw it from the figure, not from habit.

Note that the position vector only has an \(\hat e_r\) part: \(\vec r = r\,\hat e_r\). You get from O to P by going a distance \(r\) along the radial line, with no sideways part.

3. Why \(\hat e_r = \cos\theta\,\hat i + \sin\theta\,\hat j\) and \(\hat e_\theta = -\sin\theta\,\hat i + \cos\theta\,\hat j\)

This is the trig that makes the whole system work, and it is the bit worth understanding properly.

\(\hat e_r\): basic SOH-CAH-TOA

\(\hat e_r\) has length 1 and makes angle \(\theta\) with the x-axis. In the right-angled triangle it forms, the side next to \(\theta\) is \(1\cdot\cos\theta\) and the side opposite is \(1\cdot\sin\theta\). So

\[ \hat e_r = \cos\theta\,\hat i + \sin\theta\,\hat j \]

\(\hat e_\theta\): the same arrow turned a quarter-turn

\(\hat e_\theta\) is \(\hat e_r\) rotated by \(+90^\circ\), so it points at angle \(\theta + 90^\circ\). Use the same SOH-CAH-TOA with that angle, and the two “angle plus 90°” identities:

\[ \begin{gathered} \cos(\theta + 90^\circ) = -\sin\theta \\[4pt] \sin(\theta + 90^\circ) = \cos\theta \end{gathered} \]
\[ \hat e_\theta = -\sin\theta\,\hat i + \cos\theta\,\hat j \]
See it without any identities

Look at the figure in Section 2. When \(\hat e_r\) tilts up by \(\theta\), the perpendicular arrow \(\hat e_\theta\) tilts back past the vertical by the same \(\theta\). Leaning back means its horizontal part points left, so it is negative, and its size is \(\sin\theta\). Its vertical part points up with size \(\cos\theta\). That is where the minus sign comes from: \(\hat e_\theta\) leans backwards.

Two quick checks you can do in the exam

  • Perpendicular? \(\hat e_r\cdot\hat e_\theta = \cos\theta(-\sin\theta) + \sin\theta\cos\theta = 0\). Yes.
  • Length 1? \((-\sin\theta)^2 + \cos^2\theta = 1\). Yes.

It is the same pattern as Part I’s Chapter 1 trick: turning any vector \((a, b)\) a quarter-turn anticlockwise gives \((-b, a)\). Apply it to \((\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\) and you get \((-\sin\theta, \cos\theta)\).

4. How the unit vectors change (the key fact)

\(\hat i\) and \(\hat j\) never change, so their derivatives are zero. \(\hat e_r\) and \(\hat e_\theta\) depend on \(\theta\), so they change whenever \(\theta\) changes. Differentiate the component forms from Section 3 with respect to \(\theta\):

\[ \begin{gathered} \frac{d\hat e_r}{d\theta} = -\sin\theta\,\hat i + \cos\theta\,\hat j = \hat e_\theta \\[4pt] \frac{d\hat e_\theta}{d\theta} = -\cos\theta\,\hat i - \sin\theta\,\hat j = -\hat e_r \end{gathered} \]

Then the chain rule turns “per unit of θ” into “per second”, which multiplies by \(\dot\theta\):

\[ \begin{gathered} \dot{\hat e}_r = \dot\theta\,\hat e_\theta \\[4pt] \dot{\hat e}_\theta = -\dot\theta\,\hat e_r \end{gathered} \]
Remember it like this

A turning unit arrow changes in the direction it is being swung, at the rate it is being swung. \(\hat e_r\) is swung towards \(\hat e_\theta\). \(\hat e_\theta\) is swung towards \(-\hat e_r\). Both at rate \(\dot\theta\).

5. Velocity, derived slowly

Start from the position \(\vec r = r\,\hat e_r\) and differentiate. It is a product (\(r\) times \(\hat e_r\)), and both parts change with time, so use the product rule:

\[ \vec v = \frac{d}{dt}\big(r\,\hat e_r\big) = \dot r\,\hat e_r + r\,\dot{\hat e}_r = \dot r\,\hat e_r + r\,(\dot\theta\,\hat e_\theta) \]
\[ \begin{gathered} \vec v = \dot r\,\hat e_r + r\dot\theta\,\hat e_\theta \\[4pt] v_r = \dot r,\quad v_\theta = r\dot\theta,\quad v = \sqrt{\dot r^2 + (r\dot\theta)^2} \end{gathered} \]
  • \(v_r = \dot r\): how fast the distance from O changes. It is the slider moving along the arm.
  • \(v_\theta = r\dot\theta\): being carried round by the arm. A point at radius \(r\) on something turning at \(\dot\theta\) moves at \(r\dot\theta\) (Part I, Chapter 6, \(v = \omega r\)).

Direction. The angle between \(\vec v\) and the radial line is \(\psi = \tan^{-1}(v_\theta / v_r)\), using the signs to pick the quadrant. Measured from the x-axis, the direction is \(\theta + \psi\), because \(\hat e_r\) itself points at \(\theta\).

6. Acceleration, derived slowly

Differentiate \(\vec v = \dot r\,\hat e_r + r\dot\theta\,\hat e_\theta\) one term at a time. Each term is a product, so each gives two pieces.

\[ \frac{d}{dt}\big(\dot r\,\hat e_r\big) = \ddot r\,\hat e_r + \dot r\,\dot{\hat e}_r = \ddot r\,\hat e_r + \color[rgb]{0.753,0.082,0.298}{\dot r\dot\theta\,\hat e_\theta} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{d}{dt}\big(r\dot\theta\,\hat e_\theta\big) &= \color[rgb]{0.753,0.082,0.298}{\dot r\dot\theta\,\hat e_\theta} + r\ddot\theta\,\hat e_\theta + r\dot\theta\,\dot{\hat e}_\theta \\ &= \color[rgb]{0.753,0.082,0.298}{\dot r\dot\theta\,\hat e_\theta} + r\ddot\theta\,\hat e_\theta - r\dot\theta^2\,\hat e_r \end{aligned} \]

(The middle line uses the product rule on three factors: \(r\), \(\dot\theta\) and \(\hat e_\theta\) all change.) Now collect the \(\hat e_r\) parts and the \(\hat e_\theta\) parts. The two highlighted \(\dot r\dot\theta\) terms come from different places and add up, which is where the famous “2” comes from:

\[ \vec a = \big(\ddot r - r\dot\theta^2\big)\,\hat e_r + \big(r\ddot\theta + 2\dot r\dot\theta\big)\,\hat e_\theta \]

What each of the four terms means

TermWhere it comes fromPlain meaning
\(\ddot r\)\(\dot r\) changingThe slide along the arm is speeding up or slowing down.
\(-r\dot\theta^2\)\(\hat e_\theta\) turningCentripetal: anything carried round O needs an acceleration towards O, just like \(v^2/\rho\) on a circle.
\(r\ddot\theta\)\(\dot\theta\) changingThe arm’s spin is speeding up or slowing down (like \(a_t = \alpha r\)).
\(2\dot r\dot\theta\)two places, one eachCoriolis. Moving outward on a spinning arm means (1) you reach a bigger radius, where the same spin needs a faster sideways speed, and (2) your outward velocity is itself being swung round. Each effect gives one \(\dot r\dot\theta\).
Say it out loud

“a-r is r-double-dot minus r theta-dot squared. a-theta is r theta-double-dot plus two r-dot theta-dot.” Saying it ten times is genuinely the fastest way to have it on tap in the exam.

7. Special cases you should recognise

  • \(r\) constant (moving on a circle about O): \(\dot r = \ddot r = 0\), so \(\vec v = r\dot\theta\,\hat e_\theta\) and \(\vec a = -r\dot\theta^2\,\hat e_r + r\ddot\theta\,\hat e_\theta\). These are exactly the n–t results \(a_n = r\omega^2\) (towards the centre) and \(a_t = r\alpha\). Polar contains circular motion as a special case.
  • \(\theta\) constant (moving straight out along a fixed line): \(\vec v = \dot r\,\hat e_r\) and \(\vec a = \ddot r\,\hat e_r\). Plain straight-line motion.
  • Constant velocity in a straight line that does not pass through O: \(\vec a = 0\), but that does not make \(\ddot r\) or \(\ddot\theta\) zero. Setting both components to zero gives \(\ddot r = r\dot\theta^2\) and \(\ddot\theta = -2\dot r\dot\theta / r\). L06 Examples 1 and 2 are built on this.
  • A circle that passes through O (\(r = 2a\cos\theta\)): with constant \(\dot\theta\), the speed is constant at \(2a\dot\theta\) (L07 Example 4).

8. Switching between x–y and r–θ

Often a question gives you a velocity in x–y form (a piston moving horizontally, a crane tip with known \(v_x, v_y\), a plane flying level) and wants polar answers. The component of any vector along a unit vector is its dot product with that unit vector:

\[ \begin{gathered} v_r = \vec v\cdot\hat e_r = v_x\cos\theta + v_y\sin\theta \\[4pt] v_\theta = \vec v\cdot\hat e_\theta = -v_x\sin\theta + v_y\cos\theta \end{gathered} \]

Going back the other way:

\[ \begin{gathered} v_x = v_r\cos\theta - v_\theta\sin\theta \\[4pt] v_y = v_r\sin\theta + v_\theta\cos\theta \\[4pt] r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2},\quad \tan\theta = \frac{y}{x} \end{gathered} \]

Once you have \(v_r\) and \(v_\theta\), read off \(\dot r = v_r\) and \(\dot\theta = v_\theta / r\). This one move solves L06 Ex1, L06 Ex2, L07 Ex1 and Tut02 Q5.

9. The method: the six-number table

Every polar question comes down to filling in six numbers at the instant asked about. Then you substitute into two formulas.

valueraterate of rate
distance\(r\)\(\dot r\)\(\ddot r\)
angle\(\theta\)\(\dot\theta\)\(\ddot\theta\)

Where the six numbers come from

  1. Given as functions of time (\(r(t)\), \(\theta(t)\)): differentiate each twice, then put in the time (L06 Ex3, L07 Ex2, Tut02 Q3).
  2. Path given as \(r = f(\theta)\): use the chain rule.
    \[ \begin{gathered} \dot r = f'(\theta)\,\dot\theta \\[4pt] \ddot r = f''(\theta)\,\dot\theta^2 + f'(\theta)\,\ddot\theta \end{gathered} \]
    The second one is the product rule on \(f'(\theta)\,\dot\theta\): both factors change with time. Used in L07 Ex3, L07 Ex4 and Tut02 Q4.
  3. Geometry plus known Cartesian motion (a pin sliding horizontally at a fixed height, for example): find \(r\) from the geometry and \(\dot r, \dot\theta\) by projection (Section 8). If \(\vec a\) is known (often zero), set \(a_r\) and \(a_\theta\) equal to its components and solve for \(\ddot r\) and \(\ddot\theta\) (L06 Ex1, Ex2).
  4. A known speed or acceleration magnitude: write \(v^2 = \dot r^2 + (r\dot\theta)^2\) or \(a^2 = a_r^2 + a_\theta^2\) and solve for the missing number (L07 Ex3).
Traps
  • Angles must be in radians: deg/s × π/180 = rad/s.
  • Do not mix up \(\dot\theta\) and \(\ddot\theta\) when substituting (a real memo slip in your review lecture made exactly this mistake).
  • Signs mean directions: a negative \(\dot r\) means moving towards O, and a negative \(a_\theta\) means pointing opposite to \(\hat e_\theta\).

10. Lecture 6 examples, worked slowly

L06 Example 1: the hydraulic cylinder

Read the picture. The arm turns about O. Pin A slides in the arm’s slot, and the piston pushes A horizontally to the left at a constant 3.0 m/s. The piston rod is 0.50 m above O. θ is measured anticlockwise from the horizontal, and \(r = OA\).

  1. Find \(r\) from geometry. A is always 0.50 m above O, so \(r\sin\theta = 0.5\) and \(r = 0.5/\sin60^\circ = 0.5774\) m.
  2. Velocity in x–y. \(\vec v = -3.0\,\hat i\) m/s (left), so \(v_x = -3\), \(v_y = 0\).
  3. Project onto \(\hat e_r\) and \(\hat e_\theta\) (Section 8):
    \[ \begin{gathered} \dot r = v_r = (-3)\cos60^\circ = -1.50\ \text{m/s} \\[4pt] r\dot\theta = v_\theta = -(-3)\sin60^\circ = 2.598\ \text{m/s} \end{gathered} \]
    A moves left and slightly towards O, so \(r\) shrinks (negative \(\dot r\)). The arm swings anticlockwise (positive \(\dot\theta\)), getting steeper.
  4. \(\dot\theta = 2.598 / 0.5774 = 4.50\) rad/s.
  5. Acceleration. The velocity is constant, so \(\vec a = 0\). Both polar components must be zero:
    \[ a_r = \ddot r - r\dot\theta^2 = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \ddot r = r\dot\theta^2 = 0.5774(4.50)^2 = 11.69\ \text{m/s}^2 \]\[ a_\theta = r\ddot\theta + 2\dot r\dot\theta = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \ddot\theta = -\frac{2\dot r\dot\theta}{r} = -\frac{2(-1.5)(4.50)}{0.5774} = 23.38\ \text{rad/s}^2 \]
    Zero acceleration does not mean \(\ddot r\) and \(\ddot\theta\) are zero. The polar terms have to cancel each other exactly.

ṙ = −1.50 m/s, θ̇ = 4.50 rad/s, r̈ = 11.7 m/s², θ̈ = 23.4 rad/s²

L06 Example 2: the hydraulic actuator and slotted bar

Read the picture. P is pushed straight up at a constant 2 m/s, at a fixed horizontal distance \(x = 2\) m from the pivot. The slotted bar turns to follow P. Find the polar velocity and acceleration, and the bar’s angular velocity, at \(\theta = 35^\circ\).

  1. Geometry: \(y = 2\tan35^\circ = 1.40\) m and \(r = \sqrt{2^2 + 1.40^2} = 2.44\) m.
  2. Project \(\vec v = 2\hat j\): \(\dot r = v_r = 2\sin35^\circ = 1.15\) m/s and \(r\dot\theta = v_\theta = 2\cos35^\circ = 1.64\) m/s.
  3. \(\dot\theta = 1.64 / 2.44 = 0.671\) rad/s, so \(\vec v = 1.15\,\hat e_r + 1.64\,\hat e_\theta\) m/s.
  4. Acceleration: constant velocity, so \(\vec a = 0\). Then \(\ddot r = r\dot\theta^2 = 1.10\) m/s² and \(\ddot\theta = -2\dot r\dot\theta / r = -0.631\) rad/s².
  5. Check by the lecture’s route: \(y = 2\tan\theta\), so \(\dot y = 2\sec^2\theta\,\dot\theta\), giving \(\dot\theta = \cos^2\theta = 0.671\). Differentiating again: \(\ddot\theta = -2\sin\theta\cos\theta\,\dot\theta = -0.631\) rad/s². Same answer.\(\dot\theta = \cos^2\theta\) shrinks as θ grows, so the bar’s spin is slowing down. That is why \(\ddot\theta\) is negative.

v = 1.15 er + 1.64 eθ m/s, θ̇ = 0.671 rad/s, a = 0 (r̈ = 1.10 m/s², θ̈ = −0.631 rad/s²)

Sign on the slide

The last line of the lecture solution writes \(\ddot\theta = 0.631\) rad/s². Its own formula, \(-2\tan\theta\,\dot\theta^2\), gives −0.631. The bar is slowing, so the minus belongs there.

L06 Example 3: the collar with r(t) and θ(t)

Read the picture. Both coordinates are given as functions of time: \(r = 1 + 0.2t^2\) m and \(\theta = 2t\) rad. This is route 1 of the six-number method.

  1. Differentiate: \(\dot r = 0.4t\), \(\ddot r = 0.4\); \(\dot\theta = 2\), \(\ddot\theta = 0\).
  2. Six numbers at \(t = 2\) s: \(r = 1.8\), \(\dot r = 0.8\), \(\ddot r = 0.4\); \(\theta = 4\) rad, \(\dot\theta = 2\), \(\ddot\theta = 0\).
  3. Velocity: \(v_r = 0.8\) and \(v_\theta = 1.8(2) = 3.6\), so \(v = \sqrt{0.8^2 + 3.6^2} = 3.69\) m/s.
  4. Acceleration: \(a_r = 0.4 - 1.8(2)^2 = -6.8\) and \(a_\theta = 1.8(0) + 2(0.8)(2) = 3.2\), so \(a = \sqrt{6.8^2 + 3.2^2} = 7.52\) m/s².The spin is constant (\(\ddot\theta = 0\)), but \(a_\theta\) is not zero. The Coriolis term \(2\dot r\dot\theta\) comes from sliding outward while spinning.

v = 3.69 m/s, a = 7.52 m/s²

11. Lecture 7 examples, worked slowly

L07 Example 1: the crane (Cartesian to polar)

  1. Six numbers needed: only \(r = 3\) m and \(\theta = 30^\circ\) (plus the given \(v_x = 2\), \(v_y = 8\) m/s).
  2. Project (Section 8):
    \[ \begin{gathered} v_r = 2\cos30^\circ + 8\sin30^\circ = 5.73\ \text{m/s} \\[4pt] v_\theta = -2\sin30^\circ + 8\cos30^\circ = 5.93\ \text{m/s} \end{gathered} \]
  3. Angular velocity of the boom: \(v_\theta = r\dot\theta\), so \(\dot\theta = 5.93/3 = 1.98\) rad/s.The boom is the radial line. How fast it turns is exactly \(\dot\theta\).
  4. Check: \(\sqrt{5.73^2 + 5.93^2} = \sqrt{2^2 + 8^2} = 8.25\) m/s. A vector’s length does not depend on which axes you use.

v = 5.73 er + 5.93 eθ m/s, θ̇ = 1.98 rad/s

L07 Example 2: slider pulled in on a rotating arm (with directions)

  1. Functions of time: \(\theta = 0.8t - t^2/20\), so \(\dot\theta = 0.8 - 0.1t\) and \(\ddot\theta = -0.1\). \(r = 1.6 - 0.2t\), so \(\dot r = -0.2\) and \(\ddot r = 0\).
  2. At \(t = 4\) s: \(\theta = 2.4\) rad (137.5°), \(\dot\theta = 0.4\), \(\ddot\theta = -0.1\); \(r = 0.8\), \(\dot r = -0.2\), \(\ddot r = 0\).
  3. Velocity: \(\vec v = -0.2\,\hat e_r + 0.8(0.4)\,\hat e_\theta = -0.2\,\hat e_r + 0.32\,\hat e_\theta\), so \(v = 0.377\) m/s.
  4. Direction from the x-axis. \(\hat e_r\) points at θ = 137.5°, and \(\hat e_\theta\) points at θ + 90° = 227.5°. The velocity is mostly along \(\hat e_\theta\), leaning back towards \(-\hat e_r\) by \(\beta = \tan^{-1}(0.2/0.32) = 32.0^\circ\). So its angle is \(137.5^\circ + 90^\circ + 32.0^\circ = 259.5^\circ\), pointing almost straight down.General rule: angle of a polar vector = θ + atan2(\(v_\theta\), \(v_r\)).
  5. Acceleration: \(a_r = 0 - 0.8(0.4)^2 = -0.128\) and \(a_\theta = 0.8(-0.1) + 2(-0.2)(0.4) = -0.240\), so \(a = 0.272\) m/s².
  6. Direction: both components are negative, so \(\vec a\) points back from \(-\hat e_\theta\) towards \(-\hat e_r\): angle \(= 137.5^\circ - 90^\circ - \tan^{-1}(0.128/0.240) = 137.5 - 90 - 28.1 = 19.4^\circ\).

v = 0.377 m/s at 260° (≈ −100°) from x; a = 0.272 m/s² at 19.4° from x

L07 Example 3: the cardioid and the forked rod

  1. Path given as \(r = f(\theta)\), so use the chain rule:
    \[ r = 0.15(1 - \cos\theta) \quad \dot r = 0.15\sin\theta\,\dot\theta \quad \ddot r = 0.15\cos\theta\,\dot\theta^2 + 0.15\sin\theta\,\ddot\theta \]
  2. At \(\theta = 180^\circ\): \(\sin\theta = 0\) and \(\cos\theta = -1\), so \(r = 0.30\) m, \(\dot r = 0\) and \(\ddot r = -0.15\dot\theta^2\).This is the trig making life easy: \(\sin180^\circ = 0\) wipes out every term with \(\ddot\theta\) or \(\dot r\) in it.
  3. Speed gives \(\dot\theta\): \(v = \sqrt{\dot r^2 + (r\dot\theta)^2} = 0.3\dot\theta = 1.2\), so \(\dot\theta = 4\) rad/s.
  4. Acceleration magnitude gives \(\ddot\theta\): \(a_r = \ddot r - r\dot\theta^2 = -0.15(16) - 0.3(16) = -7.2\) m/s², and \(a_\theta = r\ddot\theta + 0 = 0.3\ddot\theta\).
    \[ 9^2 = 7.2^2 + (0.3\ddot\theta)^2 \;\Rightarrow\; 0.3\ddot\theta = 5.4 \;\Rightarrow\; \ddot\theta = 18\ \text{rad/s}^2 \]

θ̇ = 4 rad/s, θ̈ = 18 rad/s²

L07 Example 4: pin on a circle through O

  1. Path: \(r = 2a\cos\theta\), with \(\dot\theta\) constant (\(\ddot\theta = 0\)).
  2. Chain rule: \(\dot r = -2a\sin\theta\,\dot\theta\) and \(\ddot r = -2a\cos\theta\,\dot\theta^2\).
  3. Velocity: \(v_r = -2a\sin\theta\,\dot\theta\) and \(v_\theta = r\dot\theta = 2a\cos\theta\,\dot\theta\), so
    \[ v = 2a\dot\theta\sqrt{\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta} = 2a\dot\theta \]
    The identity \(\sin^2 + \cos^2 = 1\) is what collapses the answer. In polar questions, look for it whenever sines and cosines appear squared.
  4. Acceleration: \(a_r = -2a\cos\theta\,\dot\theta^2 - 2a\cos\theta\,\dot\theta^2 = -4a\cos\theta\,\dot\theta^2\) and \(a_\theta = 0 + 2(-2a\sin\theta\,\dot\theta)\dot\theta = -4a\sin\theta\,\dot\theta^2\), so \(a = 4a\dot\theta^2\).
  5. Sanity check with n–t: B moves round a circle of radius \(a\) at constant speed \(2a\dot\theta\), so \(a = v^2/a = 4a\dot\theta^2\). Same answer.

v = 2aθ̇, a = 4aθ̇² (both constant)

12. Tutorial 2 polar questions (no memo, so worked here)

Tut02 Q3: oscillating rod with a sliding collar

\(\theta = \tfrac{3}{\pi}\sin(\pi t)\) and \(r = 6(1 - e^{-2t})\). At \(t = 1.00\) s find the collar’s speed, its acceleration, and its acceleration relative to the rod.

  1. Differentiate: \(\dot\theta = 3\cos(\pi t)\) and \(\ddot\theta = -3\pi\sin(\pi t)\); \(\dot r = 12e^{-2t}\) and \(\ddot r = -24e^{-2t}\).
  2. At \(t = 1\): \(\theta = 0\), \(\dot\theta = -3\) rad/s, \(\ddot\theta = 0\); \(r = 5.188\) m, \(\dot r = 1.624\) m/s, \(\ddot r = -3.248\) m/s².θ is measured from the vertical here, so \(\hat e_\theta\) points the way that angle opens. Signs still work exactly as before.
  3. Speed: \(v = \sqrt{1.624^2 + (5.188\times3)^2} = 15.6\) m/s.
  4. Acceleration: \(a_r = -3.248 - 5.188(9) = -49.9\) and \(a_\theta = 0 + 2(1.624)(-3) = -9.74\), so \(a = 50.9\) m/s².
  5. Relative to the rod: someone riding on the rod only sees the sliding, \(\ddot r\,\hat e_r = -3.25\,\hat e_r\) m/s².

v = 15.6 m/s, a = 50.9 m/s², arel = −3.25 er m/s² (matches the answer sheet)

Tut02 Q4: peg driven along a spiral

The slotted link turns at a constant \(\dot\theta = 3\) rad/s and drives peg P along the spiral \(r = 0.4\theta\). Find the velocity and acceleration as it leaves the slot at \(r = 0.5\) m.

  1. Chain rule: \(\dot r = 0.4\dot\theta = 1.2\) m/s and \(\ddot r = 0.4\ddot\theta = 0\).
  2. At \(r = 0.5\): \(\theta = 1.25\) rad (not needed for magnitudes).
  3. Velocity: \(v_r = 1.2\) and \(v_\theta = 0.5(3) = 1.5\), so \(v = 1.92\) m/s.
  4. Acceleration: \(a_r = 0 - 0.5(9) = -4.5\) and \(a_\theta = 0 + 2(1.2)(3) = 7.2\), so \(a = 8.49\) m/s².

v = 1.92 m/s, a = 8.49 m/s² (matches the answer sheet)

Tut02 Q5: radar tracking a plane at the bottom of a loop

At the bottom of a vertical loop (ρ = 1000 m) a plane flies horizontally at 462 m/s and speeds up at 10 m/s². The radar is at O with \(r_x = 2400\) m and \(r_y = 1800\) m. Find \(r, \dot r, \ddot r, \theta, \dot\theta, \ddot\theta\).

  1. Geometry: \(r = \sqrt{2400^2 + 1800^2} = 3000\) m and \(\theta = \tan^{-1}(1800/2400) = 36.9^\circ\).
  2. Cartesian motion: \(\vec v = 462\,\hat i\). Acceleration: \(a_t = 10\) along the flight (\(\hat i\)), and \(a_n = v^2/\rho = 462^2/1000 = 213.4\) up towards the loop’s centre. So \(\vec a = 10\,\hat i + 213.4\,\hat j\).
  3. Project the velocity: \(\dot r = 462\cos36.9^\circ = 370\) m/s and \(r\dot\theta = -462\sin36.9^\circ = -277\) m/s, so \(\dot\theta = -0.0924\) rad/s.
  4. Project the acceleration: \(a_r = 10(0.8) + 213.4(0.6) = 136.1\) and \(a_\theta = -10(0.6) + 213.4(0.8) = 164.8\) m/s².
  5. Solve for the dot-dots: \(\ddot r = a_r + r\dot\theta^2 = 136.1 + 25.6 = 161.7\) m/s², and \(\ddot\theta = (a_\theta - 2\dot r\dot\theta)/r = (164.8 + 68.3)/3000 = 0.0777\) rad/s².

r = 3000 m, ṙ = 370 m/s, r̈ = 162 m/s², θ = 36.9°, θ̇ = −0.0924 rad/s, θ̈ = 0.0777 rad/s²

Those values come out exactly if the normal acceleration is taken as 40 m/s² (as if \(v = 200\) m/s were used in \(v^2/\rho\)). 13. Cylindrical coordinates (L08)

Cylindrical coordinates are polar coordinates with a height added. The point P is described by \(r\) and \(\theta\) in the horizontal plane, and \(z\) above it.

\[ \begin{gathered} \vec R = r\,\hat e_r + z\,\hat k \\[4pt] \vec v = \dot r\,\hat e_r + r\dot\theta\,\hat e_\theta + \dot z\,\hat k \\[4pt] \vec a = (\ddot r - r\dot\theta^2)\,\hat e_r + (r\ddot\theta + 2\dot r\dot\theta)\,\hat e_\theta + \ddot z\,\hat k \end{gathered} \]
Why nothing new appears

\(\hat k\) never changes direction, so \(z\hat k\) differentiates simply to \(\dot z\hat k\) and \(\ddot z\hat k\). All the turning happens in the horizontal plane, and that is exactly polar coordinates. Do the polar part as before and add the \(z\) part.

Magnitudes: \(v = \sqrt{v_r^2 + v_\theta^2 + v_z^2}\) and \(a = \sqrt{a_r^2 + a_\theta^2 + a_z^2}\).

L08 Example 1: the extending robot arm

  1. Functions of time: the arm extends at \(\dot r = 1.5\) from \(r = 3\), so \(r = 3 + 1.5t\), \(\ddot r = 0\). \(\theta = 0.5t\), so \(\dot\theta = 0.5\), \(\ddot\theta = 0\). \(z = 4t^2\), so \(\dot z = 8t\), \(\ddot z = 8\).
  2. At \(t = 3\) s: \(r = 7.5\), \(\dot r = 1.5\), \(\ddot r = 0\); \(\theta = 1.5\) rad, \(\dot\theta = 0.5\), \(\ddot\theta = 0\); \(z = 36\), \(\dot z = 24\), \(\ddot z = 8\).Nine numbers now (the six polar ones plus three for z). A neat table, as on the lecture slide, prevents mistakes.
  3. Velocity: \(\vec v = 1.5\,\hat e_r + 7.5(0.5)\,\hat e_\theta + 24\,\hat k = 1.5\,\hat e_r + 3.75\,\hat e_\theta + 24\,\hat k\), so \(v = 24.3\) m/s.
  4. Acceleration: \(a_r = 0 - 7.5(0.25) = -1.875\), \(a_\theta = 0 + 2(1.5)(0.5) = 1.5\), \(a_z = 8\), so \(a = 8.35\) m/s².

v = 24.3 m/s, a = 8.35 m/s²

14. Spherical coordinates (L08)

Spherical coordinates suit anything that points into the sky from a fixed spot, like a radar antenna or a fire-truck ladder. They use the straight-line distance \(R\) from O, the compass angle \(\theta\) in the horizontal plane, and the elevation angle \(\phi\) up from the horizontal (like latitude).

Velocity, and why \(\cos\phi\) appears

\[ \vec v = \dot R\,\hat e_R + R\dot\theta\cos\phi\,\hat e_\theta + R\dot\phi\,\hat e_\phi \]
  • \(v_R = \dot R\): moving along the beam (like \(\dot r\) in polar).
  • \(v_\phi = R\dot\phi\): swinging up in the vertical plane, on a circle of radius \(R\) (like \(r\dot\theta\) in polar).
  • \(v_\theta = (R\cos\phi)\,\dot\theta\): when \(\theta\) changes, A circles the vertical axis on a horizontal circle. That circle’s radius is the horizontal distance from the axis, \(R\cos\phi\) (the dashed circle in the figure). Radius × angular speed gives \(R\cos\phi\,\dot\theta\).
Sanity check

At \(\phi = 90^\circ\) (pointing straight up), \(\cos\phi = 0\). Spinning about the vertical axis then does not move A at all, and the formula agrees.

Acceleration, built from two polar pictures

\[ \begin{gathered} a_R = \ddot R - R\dot\phi^2 - R\dot\theta^2\cos^2\phi \\[4pt] a_\theta = \frac{\cos\phi}{R}\frac{d}{dt}\big(R^2\dot\theta\big) - 2R\dot\theta\dot\phi\sin\phi \\[4pt] a_\phi = \frac{1}{R}\frac{d}{dt}\big(R^2\dot\phi\big) + R\dot\theta^2\sin\phi\cos\phi \end{gathered} \]

Expanded, as on the L08 ladder slide:

\[ \begin{gathered} a_\theta = 2\dot R\dot\theta\cos\phi + R\ddot\theta\cos\phi - 2R\dot\theta\dot\phi\sin\phi \\[4pt] a_\phi = 2\dot R\dot\phi + R\ddot\phi + R\dot\theta^2\sin\phi\cos\phi \end{gathered} \]
How to rebuild these instead of memorising them

Picture 1, the vertical plane (R, φ): this is ordinary polar with \(r \to R\) and \(\theta \to \phi\). It gives \(\ddot R - R\dot\phi^2\) in \(a_R\), and \(R\ddot\phi + 2\dot R\dot\phi\) in \(a_\phi\).

Picture 2, the horizontal circle (radius \(\rho = R\cos\phi\), angle θ): this is also polar. Its transverse part \(\rho\ddot\theta + 2\dot\rho\dot\theta\), with \(\dot\rho = \dot R\cos\phi - R\dot\phi\sin\phi\), is exactly \(a_\theta\). Its centripetal part \(\rho\dot\theta^2\) points horizontally towards the vertical axis. That direction is split between \(-\hat e_R\) (a share \(\cos\phi\)) and \(+\hat e_\phi\) (a share \(\sin\phi\)), which gives the \(-R\dot\theta^2\cos^2\phi\) in \(a_R\) and the \(+R\dot\theta^2\sin\phi\cos\phi\) in \(a_\phi\).

So spherical is two polar problems glued together. If you know polar, you can rebuild every term.

Turning cylindrical components into spherical ones

Same trick as Section 8, but now the turn is by \(\phi\) in the vertical plane:

\[ \begin{gathered} u_R = u_r\cos\phi + u_z\sin\phi \\[4pt] u_\phi = -u_r\sin\phi + u_z\cos\phi \\[4pt] u_\theta \text{ unchanged} \end{gathered} \]

L08 Example 2: radar antenna tracking a jet

Read the picture. The jet flies level, parallel to the x-axis, along a line a horizontal distance \(b = 2\) km from the x-axis, at height \(h = 1750\) m above P, with \(u = 500\) km/h = 138.9 m/s. The antenna’s compass angle is \(\theta = 60^\circ\).

  1. Horizontal distance from P to below the jet: \(r = b/\sin\theta = 2000/0.866 = 2309\) m. Then \(\phi = \tan^{-1}(h/r) = \tan^{-1}(1750/2309) = 37.2^\circ\), and \(R = \sqrt{2309^2 + 1750^2} = 2898\) m.
  2. Step 1: x–y to horizontal polar (r, θ, z). \(\vec u = u\,\hat i\), so \(u_r = u\cos\theta = 69.4\), \(u_\theta = -u\sin\theta = -120.3\), \(u_z = 0\) m/s.This is Section 8 again, with \(v_x = u\) and \(v_y = 0\).
  3. Step 2: tilt up by φ to spherical. \(u_R = u_r\cos\phi = 55.4\) m/s, \(u_\phi = -u_r\sin\phi = -41.9\) m/s, and \(u_\theta = -120.3\) m/s (unchanged).
  4. Meaning of the signs: \(R\) is growing (the jet is getting further away), θ is shrinking (it is swinging towards the x-axis), and φ is shrinking (the antenna tilts down as the jet recedes). Check: \(\sqrt{55.4^2 + 120.3^2 + 41.9^2} = 138.9\) m/s. Total speed is unchanged.

|vR| = 55.4 m/s, |vθ| = 120.3 m/s, |vφ| = 41.9 m/s (so Ṙ = 55.4 m/s, θ̇ = −0.052 rad/s, φ̇ = −0.0145 rad/s)

L08 Example 3: the fire-truck ladder

  1. Nine numbers, in radians: \(R = 9 + 6 = 15\) m, \(\dot R = 0.5\), \(\ddot R = 0\); \(\dot\theta = 10^\circ/\text{s} = 0.1745\) rad/s, \(\ddot\theta = 0\); \(\phi = 30^\circ\), \(\dot\phi = 7^\circ/\text{s} = 0.1222\) rad/s, \(\ddot\phi = 0\).Converting deg/s to rad/s first is the step most often forgotten.
  2. Velocity: \(v_R = 0.5\), \(v_\theta = 15(0.1745)\cos30^\circ = 2.27\), \(v_\phi = 15(0.1222) = 1.83\), so \(v = 2.96\) m/s.
  3. Acceleration:
    \[ a_R = 0 - 15(0.1222)^2 - 15(0.1745)^2\cos^2 30^\circ = -0.567 \]\[ a_\theta = 2(0.5)(0.1745)\cos30^\circ + 0 - 2(15)(0.1745)(0.1222)\sin30^\circ = -0.169 \]\[ a_\phi = 2(0.5)(0.1222) + 0 + 15(0.1745)^2\sin30^\circ\cos30^\circ = 0.320 \]
  4. \(a = \sqrt{0.567^2 + 0.169^2 + 0.320^2}\).

15. Quick drills (answers underneath)

  1. \(r = 2\) m (constant), \(\dot\theta = 3\) rad/s (constant). Find \(\vec a\).Answer: \(a_r = -18\), \(a_\theta = 0\), so 18 m/s² towards O. (A circle at constant speed: \(r\omega^2\).)
  2. \(r = 0.5\), \(\dot r = 1\), \(\ddot r = 0\), \(\dot\theta = 2\), \(\ddot\theta = 0\). Find \(v\) and \(a\).Answer: \(\vec v = 1\,\hat e_r + 1\,\hat e_\theta\), so \(v = 1.41\) m/s. \(\vec a = -2\,\hat e_r + 4\,\hat e_\theta\), so \(a = 4.47\) m/s².
  3. A particle at \(\theta = 90^\circ\) moves with \(\vec v = 3\hat i\) m/s. What are \(v_r\) and \(v_\theta\)?Answer: \(v_r = 3\cos90^\circ = 0\) and \(v_\theta = -3\sin90^\circ = -3\). It moves purely sideways, and θ is decreasing.
  4. Spiral \(r = 3\theta\) with constant \(\dot\theta = 2\). Find \(v\) and \(a\) at \(\theta = \pi\).Answer: \(r = 9.42\), \(\dot r = 6\), \(\ddot r = 0\). \(v = \sqrt{6^2 + 18.85^2} = 19.8\) m/s. \(a_r = -37.7\) and \(a_\theta = 24\), so \(a = 44.7\) m/s².
  5. Why is it \(-\sin\theta\) in \(\hat e_\theta\)?Answer: \(\hat e_\theta\) is \(\hat e_r\) turned 90°, so it leans back past the vertical by θ and its horizontal part points left.
  6. Which term would vanish if the slider stopped sliding but the arm kept accelerating its spin?Answer: \(\dot r = 0\) kills \(2\dot r\dot\theta\). And \(\ddot r = 0\) if it stays still. What remains is \(-r\dot\theta^2\) and \(r\ddot\theta\), which is just circular motion.
  7. In spherical coordinates, why does \(v_\theta\) have \(\cos\phi\) but \(v_\phi\) does not?Answer: turning θ moves A round a horizontal circle of radius \(R\cos\phi\). Turning φ moves it round a vertical circle of full radius \(R\).

16. Formulas to know, and the one-page summary

FormulaWhat it means
\(\hat e_r = \cos\theta\,\hat i + \sin\theta\,\hat j\)Unit arrow along the radial line, away from O.
\(\hat e_\theta = -\sin\theta\,\hat i + \cos\theta\,\hat j\)\(\hat e_r\) turned 90° the way θ grows. It leans back, hence the minus.
\(\dot{\hat e}_r = \dot\theta\,\hat e_\theta\), \(\dot{\hat e}_\theta = -\dot\theta\,\hat e_r\)A turning unit arrow changes sideways, at rate \(\dot\theta\).
\(\vec v = \dot r\,\hat e_r + r\dot\theta\,\hat e_\theta\)Sliding along the arm + being carried round by it.
\(a_r = \ddot r - r\dot\theta^2\)Slide speeding up, minus the centripetal pull towards O.
\(a_\theta = r\ddot\theta + 2\dot r\dot\theta\)Spin speeding up, plus Coriolis (two \(\dot r\dot\theta\) effects).
\(v_r = v_x\cos\theta + v_y\sin\theta\), \(v_\theta = -v_x\sin\theta + v_y\cos\theta\)Dot products with \(\hat e_r\) and \(\hat e_\theta\) (x–y to polar). The same for \(\vec a\).
\(\dot r = f'\dot\theta\), \(\ddot r = f''\dot\theta^2 + f'\ddot\theta\)Chain rule for a path \(r = f(\theta)\).
Cylindrical: add \(\dot z\,\hat k\), \(\ddot z\,\hat k\)\(\hat k\) never turns, so it is just polar plus height.
\(\vec v = \dot R\,\hat e_R + R\dot\theta\cos\phi\,\hat e_\theta + R\dot\phi\,\hat e_\phi\)Along the beam, round the horizontal circle (radius \(R\cos\phi\)), up the vertical circle (radius \(R\)).
\(a_R, a_\theta, a_\phi\) (Section 14)Two polar problems glued together: the vertical plane plus the horizontal circle.

One-page summary

Draw it

  1. Radial line from O through P.
  2. \(\hat e_r\) along it, away from O.
  3. \(\hat e_\theta\) ⟂, the way θ grows.
  4. Mark the right angle.

Unit vectors

\(\hat e_r = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\)
\(\hat e_\theta = (-\sin\theta, \cos\theta)\)
\(\dot{\hat e}_r = \dot\theta\,\hat e_\theta\), \(\dot{\hat e}_\theta = -\dot\theta\,\hat e_r\)

Polar

\(v_r = \dot r\), \(v_\theta = r\dot\theta\)
\(a_r = \ddot r - r\dot\theta^2\)
\(a_\theta = r\ddot\theta + 2\dot r\dot\theta\)

x–y ↔ r–θ

\(v_r = v_x\cos\theta + v_y\sin\theta\)
\(v_\theta = -v_x\sin\theta + v_y\cos\theta\)

Method

  1. Fill the six-number table \(r, \dot r, \ddot r, \theta, \dot\theta, \ddot\theta\) (nine with z or φ).
  2. Get them from \(r(t), \theta(t)\), from \(r = f(\theta)\) by the chain rule, from geometry plus projection, or from a known magnitude.
  3. Substitute, then combine with Pythagoras.
  4. Direction = θ + atan2(\(v_\theta\), \(v_r\)).

Cylindrical

Polar + \(\dot z\,\hat k\), \(\ddot z\,\hat k\)

Spherical

\(v_R = \dot R\), \(v_\theta = R\dot\theta\cos\phi\), \(v_\phi = R\dot\phi\)
\(a_R = \ddot R - R\dot\phi^2 - R\dot\theta^2\cos^2\phi\)
\(a_\theta = 2\dot R\dot\theta\cos\phi + R\ddot\theta\cos\phi - 2R\dot\theta\dot\phi\sin\phi\)
\(a_\phi = 2\dot R\dot\phi + R\ddot\phi + R\dot\theta^2\sin\phi\cos\phi\)

Traps

  • Radians, not degrees.
  • \(\dot\theta\) vs \(\ddot\theta\) when substituting.
  • \(\vec a = 0\) does not mean \(\ddot r = 0\).
  • The “2” in \(2\dot r\dot\theta\).
  • \(\cos\phi\) only in the θ terms.
