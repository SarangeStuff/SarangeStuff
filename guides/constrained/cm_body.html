2The velocity-line trick (your sketch)

{{fig:hack_cases}}

$$v_{\text{centre}} = \frac{v_{\text{left strand}} + v_{\text{right strand}}}{2}\qquad(\text{up} = +)$$ Draw the two strand speeds as arrows on the ends of a horizontal diameter. The straight line through their tips crosses the centre at the axle's speed. Where it crosses zero is the pulley's instantaneous centre.

Why it works

The rope grips the pulley, so each rim point where a strand leaves moves with that strand. A pulley is a rigid disc, so its velocity varies linearly across a diameter (it's a wheel "rolling" on its own rope). The centre is halfway along, so it gets the average.

Rules for chaining pulleys

  1. A strand tied to the ceiling or anything fixed: v = 0.
  2. A fixed-axle pulley: centre 0, so its two strands move equal and opposite. A rope pulled down on one side rises on the other.
  3. A strand tied to a moving body (a block, a pulley's hub) moves with that body.
  4. One straight strand has one velocity along its whole length, so carry it from pulley to pulley.
  5. Start from the known input (motor, drum, pulled end) and work outwards.
  6. Accelerations: the same rule, $a_{\text{centre}} = \tfrac12(a_L + a_R)$.
  7. Only for parallel strands. If a strand is slanted, use the length method.

3The length method, step by step

  1. Datum: a fixed line, usually the ceiling or a fixed pulley's axle.
  2. Coordinates: one for every moving thing (block, moving pulley's centre, rope end), measured from the datum, positive down. Draw them: this is often a marked step.
  3. One equation per cable: add its straight segments. Arcs around pulleys are constant: drop them. A segment between two moving things has length = the difference of their coordinates.
  4. Count the segments ending on each pulley. A pulley hanging in a loop has 2; the pulley B in CT1 Q1 has 3.
  5. Differentiate once for v and twice for a. Constants vanish.
  6. Signs to words: positive = moving away from the datum (down here); negative = up.

Why the factor of 2 (or 3)

If a pulley hanging in a loop drops 1 cm, both its segments get 1 cm longer. That 2 cm has to come from the rest of the rope, so the free end moves 2 cm.

Differentiation toolkit

$\dfrac{d}{dt}(s_B - s_C) = v_B - v_C$

Slanted segment $\sqrt{x^2 + b^2}$ (b constant):

$\dfrac{d}{dt}\sqrt{x^2 + b^2} = \dfrac{x\dot x}{\sqrt{x^2 + b^2}}$

$\dfrac{d^2}{dt^2}\sqrt{x^2 + b^2} = \dfrac{\dot x^2 + x\ddot x}{\sqrt{x^2 + b^2}} - \dfrac{x^2\dot x^2}{(x^2 + b^2)^{3/2}}$

Degrees of freedom = (number of coordinates) − (number of cables) = how many motions the question must give you.

4Traps

  • Trace each cable with your finger from end to end. Mixing up which cable a segment belongs to is the most common mistake.
  • "Reels in at 100 mm/s" means the cable's length outside the drum shrinks: $\dot L = -100$ mm/s.
  • Relative velocity: $v_{B/L} = v_B - v_L$, using the same sign convention for both.
  • Constant input speed does not mean zero acceleration everywhere: with a slanted strand the crate still accelerates (S25 Q2c).
  • Give a direction with every answer (↑ ↓), not just a signed number.

{{crop:ct1q1}}

The motor reels in cable at 100 mm/s. Find (a) the velocity of the load, vL, and (b) the velocity of pulley B relative to the load, vB/L. [6 marks]

{{fig:ea4}}

Length method (datum: ceiling, y down +)

Cable 1 (green): motor → over the top-left fixed pulley → down to B → under B → up around the small fixed pulley → down to B's hub. Three segments end on B:

$L_1 = 3y_B + \text{const}$; reeled in, so $\dot L_1 = -100$ → $3\dot y_B = -100$ → $\dot y_B = -33.3$ mm/s (B rises at 33.3 mm/s)

Cable 2 (orange): B's hub → under the load pulley → ceiling:

$L_2 = (y_L - y_B) + y_L$ → $2\dot y_L = \dot y_B$ → $\dot y_L = -16.7$ mm/s

(a) vL = 16.7 mm/s ↑

(b) $v_{B/L} = \dot y_B - \dot y_L = -33.3 - (-16.7) = -16.7$ mm/s

(b) vB/L = 16.7 mm/s ↑

Velocity-line check

{{fig:ea4_lines}}
  1. The motor strand runs down into the motor at 100, so over the fixed pulley, B's left strand goes up 100.
  2. B's right strand goes up over the small fixed pulley and comes back down to B's hub. Its speed is therefore minus B's: $-v_B$.
  3. $v_B = \tfrac12(100 - v_B)$ → $3v_B = 100$ → $v_B = 33.3$ ↑ ✓
  4. Load pulley: left strand 33.3 ↑ (B's hub), right strand tied → $v_L = 16.7$ ↑ ✓

Same pattern as F25 Q2: the drum draws in 180 mm/s, giving 180/3 = 60 mm/s for the middle pulley and 30 mm/s for W, so h = 30 × 10 = 300 mm. Spot the "three segments on one pulley" layout and you have the answer in seconds.

E5S25 Q2: slanted rope, roller A moving down at 2.5 m/s

Geometry at s = 2 m (TRUE SCALE)

{{fig:ea5}}

The rope to A is slanted, so the velocity line doesn't apply. Use the length method with Pythagoras.

  1. Variables: datum at the pulley level. $x_C$ is from the pulley down to the crate; $x_A$ is from B down to A. Draw them (2 marks).
  2. Length: $l = x_C + \sqrt{x_A^2 + 3^2} = 8$ m
  3. At s = 2 m, $x_C = 5 - 2 = 3$ m, so $\sqrt{x_A^2 + 9} = 5$ and $x_A = 4$ m.
  4. Velocity: $0 = \dot x_C + \dfrac{x_A\dot x_A}{\sqrt{x_A^2 + 9}}$, so $\dot x_C = -\dfrac{4(2.5)}{5} = -2$ m/s → 2 m/s ↑
  5. Acceleration ($\ddot x_A = 0$): $\ddot x_C = -\dfrac{\dot x_A^2}{5} + \dfrac{x_A^2\dot x_A^2}{5^3} = -1.25 + 0.80 = -0.45$ m/s²
Direction check. $x_C$ is measured downwards, so −0.45 means 0.45 m/s² upwards. The memo writes "0.45 m/s² ↓". Physically, the crate's upward speed is $v_C = v_A x_A/\sqrt{x_A^2+9}$, which grows as A goes lower (1.90 → 2.00 → 2.08 m/s at xA = 3.5, 4, 4.5 m), so the crate is speeding up upwards.

Part B · n–t, polar, cylindrical and spherical as vectors

B1The one idea: unit vectors are just directions

Every coordinate system in this course is a set of unit vectors that turn as the particle moves. Write them in fixed i, j, k components and two tools do everything:

  • Dot product = projection: the component of any vector along a unit vector is $\vec v\cdot\hat e$. That's how you switch between systems.
  • Magnitude check: $|\vec a|$ must come out the same in every system. If it doesn't, a sign is wrong.
SystemUnit vectors in i, j, kVelocityAcceleration
n–t$\hat e_t$ = direction of $\vec v$; $\hat e_n$ = $\hat e_t$ turned 90° towards the centre of curvature$v\hat e_t$$\dot v\,\hat e_t + \dfrac{v^2}{\rho}\hat e_n$
Polar$\hat e_r = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$, $\;\hat e_\theta = (-\sin\theta, \cos\theta)$$\dot r\hat e_r + r\dot\theta\hat e_\theta$$(\ddot r - r\dot\theta^2)\hat e_r + (r\ddot\theta + 2\dot r\dot\theta)\hat e_\theta$
Cylindricalpolar + $\hat e_z = \vec k$add $\dot z\hat e_z$add $\ddot z\hat e_z$
Spherical (φ = elevation)$\hat e_R = (\cos\varphi\cos\theta,\ \cos\varphi\sin\theta,\ \sin\varphi)$
$\hat e_\theta = (-\sin\theta,\ \cos\theta,\ 0)$
$\hat e_\varphi = (-\sin\varphi\cos\theta,\ -\sin\varphi\sin\theta,\ \cos\varphi)$		$\dot R\hat e_R + R\dot\theta\cos\varphi\,\hat e_\theta + R\dot\varphi\,\hat e_\varphi$see B6 (formula sheet)

Where the polar formulas come from: $\dot{\hat e}_r = \dot\theta\hat e_\theta$ and $\dot{\hat e}_\theta = -\dot\theta\hat e_r$ (differentiate the components). Then $\vec r = r\hat e_r$ gives $\vec v = \dot r\hat e_r + r\dot\theta\hat e_\theta$; differentiate once more for $\vec a$.

B2n–t with vectors: S25 Q1b (aircraft with airbrakes)

Given: v = 75 m/s at β = 15° below the horizontal, moving right; ρ = 600 m, with the centre of curvature below the path; airbrakes give $a_x = -9.81$ m/s².

  1. $\hat e_t = (\cos 15°, -\sin 15°) = (0.966,\ -0.259)$, along the motion.
  2. $\hat e_n$ = $\hat e_t$ turned 90° clockwise (towards the centre, below): $(-0.259,\ -0.966)$.
  3. $a_n = v^2/\rho = 75^2/600 = 9.375$ m/s²
  4. x-component: $-9.81 = a_t(0.966) + 9.375(-0.259)$, so $a_t = -7.64$ m/s². Negative means slowing, as airbrakes should.
  5. y-component: $a_y = a_t(-0.259) + 9.375(-0.966) = +1.98 - 9.06 = -7.08$ m/s²
  6. Magnitude check: $\sqrt{a_t^2 + a_n^2} = 12.10$ and $\sqrt{9.81^2 + 7.08^2} = 12.10$ ✓

B3Polar with vectors: S25 Q1a (radar tracking)

Given: r = 800 m, θ = 45°, v = 75 m/s at 15° below the horizontal.

  1. $\vec v = 75(\cos(-15°), \sin(-15°))$
  2. $\hat e_r = (\cos 45°, \sin 45°)$, $\;\hat e_\theta = (-\sin 45°, \cos 45°)$
  3. $v_r = \vec v\cdot\hat e_r = 75\cos(45° + 15°) = 37.5$ m/s
  4. $v_\theta = \vec v\cdot\hat e_\theta = 75\sin(-15° - 45°) = -64.95$ m/s
  5. $\dot r = v_r = 37.5$ m/s; $\;\dot\theta = v_\theta/r = -64.95/800 = -0.0812$ rad/s (CW)

The dot products handle the angles and signs for you. The memo uses 30°, the angle between $\vec v$ and $-\hat e_\theta$: $v_r = v\sin 30° = v\cos 60°$ and $v_\theta = -v\cos 30°$. Same numbers; the dot product just saves you from picking the angle.

B5CT1 2025 Q3: car on a helical ramp (cylindrical coordinates), fully explained

{{crop:ct1q3}}

Helix radius 7.2 m, rising 3 m per half-turn. Speed 25 km/h, decreasing at 3 km/h per second. Find aR, aθ and az. [13 marks]

1 · Unroll the helix to get its angle

{{fig:b_helix}}

Half a turn is an arc of $r\pi = 22.62$ m in the horizontal direction while rising 3 m:

$\tan\gamma = \dfrac{3}{7.2\pi} = 0.1326 \Rightarrow \gamma = 7.56°$

The velocity is tangent to the helix: it points along the slope of the unrolled triangle.

2 · Split the speed and its rate

$v = 25/3.6 = 6.944$ m/s; $\;\dot v = -3/3.6 = -0.833$ m/s²

$v_\theta = r\dot\theta = v\cos\gamma = 6.884$ m/s → $\dot\theta = 0.956$ rad/s

$v_z = \dot z = v\sin\gamma = 0.913$ m/s; $\;\dot r = 0$ (constant radius)

Because γ is fixed, the rates split the same way: $r\ddot\theta = \dot v\cos\gamma$ and $\ddot z = \dot v\sin\gamma$.

3 · Cylindrical components

$a_R = \ddot r - r\dot\theta^2 = 0 - 7.2(0.956)^2 = $ −6.58 m/s² (towards the axis)

$a_\theta = r\ddot\theta + 2\dot r\dot\theta = \dot v\cos\gamma + 0 = $ −0.826 m/s²

$a_z = \ddot z = \dot v\sin\gamma = $ −0.110 m/s²

{{fig:b_helix_top}}

4 · Vector check with n–t

The helix's radius of curvature is $\rho = r/\cos^2\gamma = 7.327$ m, so $a_n = v^2/\rho = 6.58$ (that's −aR ✓) and $a_t = \dot v = -0.833$.

$|\vec a| = \sqrt{6.58^2 + 0.833^2} = 6.635$ and $\sqrt{6.58^2 + 0.826^2 + 0.110^2} = 6.635$ ✓

Why each term

  • $a_R$ is the centripetal part: the car circles the axis at θ̇, so $-r\dot\theta^2$ points inwards. It's there even at constant speed.
  • $a_\theta$ and $a_z$ come only from the slowing down: the tangential deceleration −0.833 splits along the helix direction into horizontal (cos γ) and vertical (sin γ) parts.
  • $2\dot r\dot\theta = 0$ because the radius never changes. Signs assume θ increases in the direction the car drives.

aR = −6.58 m/s² · aθ = −0.826 m/s² · az = −0.110 m/s²

B6Spherical with vectors

Building the unit vectors. Let $\hat e_h = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta, 0)$ be the horizontal direction under the particle. Tilt it up by φ:

$\hat e_R = \cos\varphi\,\hat e_h + \sin\varphi\,\vec k$, $\;\hat e_\varphi = -\sin\varphi\,\hat e_h + \cos\varphi\,\vec k$, $\;\hat e_\theta = (-\sin\theta, \cos\theta, 0)$

Velocity (formula sheet): $\vec v = \dot R\hat e_R + R\dot\theta\cos\varphi\,\hat e_\theta + R\dot\varphi\,\hat e_\varphi$. The θ-motion happens on a horizontal circle of radius $R\cos\varphi$, hence the cos φ.

Acceleration (formula sheet):
$a_R = \ddot R - R\dot\theta^2\cos^2\varphi - R\dot\varphi^2$
$a_\theta = 2\dot R\dot\theta\cos\varphi + R\ddot\theta\cos\varphi - 2R\dot\theta\dot\varphi\sin\varphi$
$a_\varphi = 2\dot R\dot\varphi + R\ddot\varphi + R\dot\theta^2\cos\varphi\sin\varphi$

{{fig:sph_geom}}

Worked example (my own, to practise the projections)

An aircraft flies east at 250 m/s ($\vec v = 250\vec i$). A radar sees it at R = 4000 m, θ = 30°, φ = 20°. Find Ṙ, θ̇ and φ̇.

$\hat e_R = (0.814,\ 0.470,\ 0.342)$, $\;\hat e_\theta = (-0.5,\ 0.866,\ 0)$, $\;\hat e_\varphi = (-0.296,\ -0.171,\ 0.940)$

$\dot R = \vec v\cdot\hat e_R = 203.4$ m/s

$R\dot\theta\cos\varphi = \vec v\cdot\hat e_\theta = -125$ → $\dot\theta = -0.0333$ rad/s

$R\dot\varphi = \vec v\cdot\hat e_\varphi = -74.05$ → $\dot\varphi = -0.0185$ rad/s

Check: $\sqrt{203.4^2 + 125^2 + 74.05^2} = 250$ ✓

✓Summary

  1. Pulleys: datum, coordinates, one length equation per cable; count the segments on each pulley; differentiate.
  2. Velocity line: centre = average of its two strands; tied strand = 0; fixed axle → equal and opposite. Parallel strands only.
  3. Slanted strand: Pythagoras, then the chain rule (twice for acceleration).
  4. Translate signs into ↑ ↓ using your coordinate directions.
  1. Write unit vectors in i, j, k; switch systems with dot products.
  2. n–t: $\hat e_n$ points towards the centre; a negative $a_t$ points backwards.
  3. Helix: unroll it to get γ; split v and v̇ with cos γ and sin γ.
  4. Always check |a| in two systems, and that each direction makes physical sense. These checks caught the memo slips in S25 Q1b (magnitude) and Q2c (direction).
